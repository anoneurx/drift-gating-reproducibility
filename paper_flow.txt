Drift-Aware Adaptive Page Replacement: Literature Review and TraceDriven Evaluation of a Statistically Gated Policy Controller
Hunain Tahir, Muhammad Qasim, and Hussain Ahmad
Student IDs: F2024332128, F2024332122, F2024332173
School of Systems and Technology (SST), University of Management and Technology (UMT), Lahore, Pakistan
Supervisor: Mohsin Ghouri
Abstract—Page-replacement policies decide which memory page is evicted under memory pressure.
Classical policies perform well on stable access patterns but can degrade when the workload changes
phase. This paper reviews 15 studies from 2025–2026 on adaptive replacement, statistical drift detection
and emerging memory systems, and finds that the three areas remain weakly connected: adaptive
policies rarely use an explicit statistical change decision, while drift detectors are rarely evaluated under
memory-management cost constraints. We therefore design and evaluate a drift-aware controller that
separates a cheap per-reference path from a periodic control path. It monitors the epoch miss ratio with
a CUSUM statistic, confirms a candidate change with a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test on
sampled reuse times, evaluates candidate policies by sampled shadow simulation, and switches only
when the estimated gain exceeds a transition-cost margin. In a trace-driven simulator we compare it
with eleven fixed and adaptive baselines (ten in the paired statistical comparison; MRU is reported
descriptively) and an offline optimum on 110 held-out traces (synthetic phase traces and kernel-model
composites), after freezing parameters on separate training traces and re-testing on a fresh trace set. On
80 non-stable traces the controller’s mean miss ratio was 0.410, 14.2% below LRU and significantly
below LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC (Holm-adjusted), but not better than S3-FIFO (0.402) or
a simple regret-driven LRU/MRU switcher (0.381). It detected 96% of 280 phase boundaries within
about one epoch with no false confirmations on the 30 stable traces (11 spurious confirmations outside
boundary windows occurred on the 60 non-stable detection traces), and matched continuous shadowbased switching (0.410 vs 0.407) with 81% fewer shadow evaluations. Ablations show that state
migration and shadow evaluation are essential, whereas the second-stage confirmation gave no
measurable benefit; the controller also costs about 6.4 times the per-reference time of LRU in the
simulator. The results delimit when drift-gated policy selection is useful and which design elements
matter, but do not establish superiority over the strongest existing methods.
Index Terms—Adaptive caching, change detection, concept drift, page replacement, shadow simulation,
trace-driven evaluation, virtual memory.
I. INTRODUCTION
Physical memory is finite. When a process references a
page that is not resident and no free frame is available, the
memory manager must evict a resident page. The rule that
makes this choice, the page-replacement policy, affects
page-fault frequency, storage traffic, tail latency and,
ultimately, application throughput. Recency-based policies
such as least recently used (LRU) and its CLOCK
approximation remain the practical default in operating
systems, while the research literature offers frequencyaware, scan-resistant and self-tuning alternatives, including
the adaptive replacement cache (ARC) [1], CLOCK with
adaptive replacement (CAR) [2], the low inter-reference
recency set policy (LIRS) [3], 2Q [4] and S3-FIFO [5].
No single policy is best for every access pattern. A
sequential scan can flush a cache tuned for repeated reuse,
a loop slightly larger than memory can make LRU miss on
every reference, and a policy that protects a previously hot
working set can retain pages that are useless after a phase
change. Real workloads move between such regimes: a
database alternates between index lookups and table scans,
a scientific code between computation kernels, and a
multitasking system between applications. The question
this paper studies is therefore not which policy is best, but
whether a system can recognize that the reference
behaviour has changed and respond at an acceptable cost.

The stakes of this choice have grown. Containerized and
multi-tenant servers interleave unrelated workloads on
shared memory, GPU and accelerator memory is
oversubscribed by multitasking and large-model serving,
and tiered memory systems make a wrong eviction more
expensive because the next level is slow or recomputation
is costly [26]–[28]. Recent studies show that adaptive and
learned policies can reduce misses substantially [15], [18],
[19], but also that simple policies such as LRU remain
surprisingly competitive in some settings [28] and that no
policy dominates across workload patterns [29]. A
principled account of when adaptation pays for itself is
therefore needed, not only another adaptive algorithm.
We use workload drift to mean a statistically meaningful
change over time in the distribution or temporal structure
of page references. Two research lines address related
problems. Adaptive replacement algorithms infer from
feedback which behaviour to favour: ARC shifts capacity
between recency and frequency lists, LeCaR and
CACHEUS reweight expert policies by regret, and recent
learned or learning-augmented designs reduce the cost of
learning on the eviction path [11], [12], [15], [19].
Concept-drift research, in contrast, provides formal change
detectors with controllable error rates, such as adaptive
windowing and sequential tests [6], [7], [20]–[25]. The two
lines have overlapping goals but different constraints. A
drift detector built for a machine-learning pipeline may be
1

too expensive, or rest on assumptions (labels, prediction
errors) that do not exist in a page-reference stream. A
replacement algorithm that adapts continuously may,
conversely, switch behaviour without any control over the
rate of unnecessary switches.
A closely related precedent exists outside operating-system
paging. Adaptive cache-mode selection for queries over
raw data [34] keeps a shadow cache of the alternative
mode and switches when a significance test favours it.
That work shows that statistically guided cache adaptation
is not new; what remains open is whether drift-gated
selection among page-replacement policies is effective
once the costs of monitoring, shadow evaluation and
policy-state migration are counted, and how it compares
with strong existing replacement algorithms.
Four constraints make drift-aware replacement hard. (a)
Rate. References arrive at memory speed, so any perreference statistical work is prohibitive, and monitoring
must be sampled or summarized by epoch. (b) Missing
labels and policy-dependent signals. There is no ground
truth for “the workload changed”, and the most obvious
signal, the miss ratio, depends on the active policy and
capacity, so a switch can itself create an apparent change.
(c) State. Policies keep different metadata, and a switch
must rebuild it from the resident pages; the quality of that
migration, and which pages it retains, can decide whether a
switch helps or harms. (d) Error control. False alarms
waste work and cause oscillation, while missed changes
leave a mismatched policy in place. A design that ignores
any of the four can look good on paper and fail in
operation.
This paper makes four contributions. First, we review 15
studies from 2025–2026 together with the foundational
work needed to place them, and identify where adaptive
replacement, drift detection and emerging memory systems
fail to connect (Sections III and IV). Second, we specify a
drift-aware controller with a deliberately small set of
components: a sequential monitor on the epoch miss ratio,
a distributional confirmation test on sampled reuse times,
sampled shadow simulation of a four-policy candidate set,
and a cost-aware switching rule with a recency-filtered
state migration (Section V-C). Third, we evaluate it in a
trace-driven simulator against eleven fixed and adaptive
baselines (ten in the paired tests) and an offline optimum,
using a protocol that separates training from held-out data,
freezes parameters before testing, and reports every design
amendment, including one made after a first test round
(Section V). Fourth, we report where the approach helps,
where it does not, and which components matter, through
ablations, sensitivity analyses, a sampling-validity study
and a cost analysis.
Our research questions are: RQ1 How do recent adaptive
replacement policies detect and respond to changing access
behaviour? RQ2 Which drift detectors are candidates for
low-overhead use in memory management? RQ3 What do
emerging GPU, multi-tier and benchmark studies imply for
replacement under changing workloads? RQ4 Can a driftconfirmed, cost-aware controller select among replacement
policies more effectively than fixed and heuristic-adaptive
baselines under controlled phase changes? RQ1–RQ3 are
answered by the review; RQ4 is answered empirically.

We adopt a deliberately cautious evaluation stance. The
protocol fixes the design and parameters on training traces
before test traces are touched, evaluates all methods on
identical traces, uses paired non-parametric statistics with
multiple-comparison correction, includes an offline
optimum so that the remaining gap is visible, and reports
negative and null findings together with every design
amendment. We also compare against an Acme-style
variant that applies the same shadow evaluation without a
drift gate [34], because that comparison isolates what drift
detection itself contributes. This stance is intended to make
the conclusions trustworthy even where they are
unfavourable to the proposed controller.
The answer to RQ4 is mixed, and we state it here so that
the scope of the paper is clear. On 80 held-out non-stable
traces, the controller reduced the mean miss ratio by 14.2%
relative to LRU and was significantly better than LRU,
FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC after multiplecomparison correction. It was not significantly different
from 2Q, LeCaR, S3-FIFO or a continuous-shadow
variant, and it was worse on average than a simple LPRstyle heuristic that switches between LRU and MRU on reaccess feedback. Compared with continuous shadow
evaluation, drift gating cut shadow evaluations by 81% at
equal miss ratio. The controller’s simulated per-reference
cost, however, was 6.4 times that of LRU. We therefore do
not claim superiority over the best existing methods; we
claim a measured characterization of drift-gated policy
selection, with design lessons that apply to any adaptive
replacement system (Section V-I).
The evidence has limits that readers should keep in mind.
The experiments use generated reference traces, namely
synthetic phase traces with known change points and
composite traces from five kernel-level access models, not
traces collected from running operating systems, because
no external trace repositories were accessible in the
evaluation environment. The simulator measures
algorithmic behaviour and relative cost, not kernel
performance. Section VI and Section VII discuss these
limitations in detail. The paper’s value lies in an honest,
reproducible comparison and in the specific mechanisms it
isolates, such as the effect of stale pages on policy
switching, which we expect to hold on real traces but have
not verified there.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section II gives conceptual background. Section III states
the review scope and synthesizes the reviewed studies, and
Section IV identifies the research gaps. Section V presents
the experimental methodology and results. Section VI
discusses threats to validity, Section VII the limitations
and scope of the claims, and Section VIII concludes.
II. CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND
A. Page Replacement and Workload Phases
A reference trace is an ordered sequence of page
identifiers. For a capacity of C frames, a replacement
policy chooses the victim whenever a reference misses and
all frames are occupied. LRU evicts the least recently used
page; LFU the least frequently used; FIFO the oldest;
CLOCK approximates LRU with a reference bit and a
circular scan; MRU evicts the most recently used page,
2

which is favourable for cyclic scans larger than memory.
Hybrid designs use several queues to separate one-time
from repeated references [1]–[5]. The miss ratio is the
fraction of references that miss; it is a necessary but
incomplete quality measure, since metadata cost, decision
cost and adaptation speed also matter. Results on storageblock traces are cache-replacement results and are not
direct evidence about virtual-memory paging.
Workload phases differ in working-set size, reuse-distance
distribution, sequentiality and frequency skew. Drift may
be abrupt, gradual or recurring [6]. Phase changes also
interact with policy state: a policy that retains pages of the
previous phase (for example MRU, which pins the oldest
resident pages) can perform far worse than its steady-state
behaviour suggests until that state is flushed. This
interaction is central to the design and results of this paper.

A. Adaptive and Learning-Based Replacement
3L-Cache [15] reduces the cost of learned eviction by
moving learning and inference off the critical path,
evaluated on 4,855 CDN, key-value and block traces, but
its overhead remains above LRU. LPR [16] uses re-access
feedback to switch among policies at runtime, for example
from LRU to MRU, with a heuristic rather than statistical
switching signal. EACLOCK [17] adapts a CLOCK-style
policy from hit-rate feedback for skewed and shifting
workloads. Clock2Q+ [18] targets correlated metadata
references in VMware vSAN and reports up to 28.5%
lower miss ratio than S3-FIFO on metadata traces. S4FIFO [19] learns configuration parameters for a FIFOfamily heuristic rather than every eviction, and, like 3LCache, separates a fast data path from slower control logic.

B. Workload Drift and Statistical Detection
Concept-drift research distinguishes changes in the input
distribution, in the input–output relationship, or both [6]. A
page-reference stream has no labels, so a controller must
monitor observable summaries such as the miss ratio or
reuse-time distribution. Adaptive windowing (ADWIN)
tests whether sub-windows have different means [7];
Page–Hinkley and CUSUM procedures accumulate
deviations from a reference level and signal when the sum
exceeds a threshold [30]; two-sample tests such as the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test compare distributions.
Each has assumptions about sample size, independence
and repeated testing that must be addressed in an
implementation. Because memory operations occur at very
high rates, a detector for this setting must avoid perreference heavy computation and must balance detection
delay, false alarms, memory use and runtime cost.

B. Drift Detection
PUDD [20] detects drift from prediction uncertainty but
needs a predictive model. A label-free statistical-processcontrol detector [21] stresses power and false-positive
control but is evaluated by simulation of model-monitoring
scenarios. Dynamic threshold determination [22] adapts
alarm thresholds after each alarm, at the cost of
maintaining parallel candidate models temporarily. A
transformer–autoencoder hybrid [23] and an HTM-plusSPRT framework [24] offer richer temporal modelling but
impose training or univariate-data assumptions. ADWINU [25] adapts adaptive windowing to unsupervised
settings. None is evaluated on page-reference streams or at
memory-management rates.

C. Earlier Adaptive Replacement and Cache Modelling
SEQ [8] detects long fault sequences and handles them
separately, and EELRU [9] adjusts its eviction point from
recency-distribution evidence. ARC [1] balances recency
and frequency using ghost-list hits, CAR [2] transfers the
idea to CLOCK, LIRS [3] uses inter-reference recency,
and 2Q [4] separates newly admitted from re-referenced
pages. S3-FIFO [5] shows that FIFO queues with a small
admission queue can match or beat more complex designs.
ACME [10], LeCaR [11] and CACHEUS [12] mix expert
policies using regret or learned feedback. Miniature
simulation [13] and spatially hashed sampling [14]
approximate larger cache configurations cheaply, which
makes on-demand evaluation of alternative policies
feasible, although sampling can distort temporal structure
and policy rankings. These works establish that workloadsensitive replacement is long-standing; the proposed
controller does not claim that adaptation itself is new.

C. GPU, Multi-Tier and Benchmark Studies
MSched [26] predicts the next task’s working set from
kernel launch arguments to migrate GPU memory
proactively, which relies on predictability that CPU page
streams lack. gpu_ext [27] makes GPU-driver policies
replaceable eBPF programs, an architectural mechanism
for safe policy updates. The LLM prefix-cache study [28]
finds LRU surprisingly competitive, cautioning against
assuming that sophisticated policies always win. A staletolerant cache benchmark [29] finds that no policy
dominates across four synthetic workload patterns. Finally,
Acme [34] is the closest precedent for statistical policy
switching: it simulates the alternative caching mode
through shadow entries and switches when a one-tailed ttest at the 0.05 level favours it, reporting execution time
within 16% of the optimal mode in the worst case and 4%
on average on TPC-H workloads.

III. REVIEW SCOPE AND APPROACH
This is a focused narrative review, not a systematic review
or meta-analysis. The study set was selected by the authors
to cover adaptive replacement, drift detection and
emerging memory or cache settings, and comprises 15
peer-reviewed publications and preprints from 2025–2026
plus foundational work needed to explain the lineage. One
study [20] first appeared as a preprint in December 2024

These gaps are empirical. The question is whether
statistically confirmed changes in compact page-reference
summaries, combined with on-demand candidate
evaluation and explicit switching-cost safeguards, give a
favourable trade-off against fixed policies and existing
adaptive methods. Each component of the proposed
controller has precedent (ADWIN [7], miniature
simulation [13], SHARDS [14], statistical cache-mode
selection [34]); the claim examined here concerns their
integration for page-replacement policy selection and the
measured consequences. The review cannot establish that
no related implementation exists outside the selected
literature, and the proposal is not presented as the first
statistical cache-adaptation system.

The reviewed work establishes that adaptive replacement
is well studied [1]–[5], [8]–[12]; that learning-based
policies can respond online to access patterns [15]–[19];
that statistical drift detection offers explicit change
evidence [6], [7], [20]–[25]; and that cache-mode selection
has used shadow evaluation and significance testing [34].

V. EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY AND RESULTS
A. Hypotheses and Protocol Integrity
We test four hypotheses derived from RQ4. H1
(replacement quality): on workloads with phase changes
the controller achieves a lower mean miss ratio than the
strongest fixed baseline, while not being worse on stable
workloads. H2 (switch quality): relative to heuristicadaptive baselines, drift-gated switching avoids
unnecessary switches without unacceptable detection
delay. H3 (overhead): the controller’s total added cost
stays within a modest budget relative to LRU or CLOCK.
H4 (sampling validity): candidate rankings from sampled
4

shadow simulation agree with full-capacity simulation
often enough to support switching. No numerical budget
for H3 or agreement threshold for H4 was fixed before the
experiments. We therefore classify the analyses as follows.
H1 and H2 are confirmatory only in direction: the margin
for “not worse” on stable traces and a switch-count
criterion were not fixed in advance, and the strongest
baseline was identified only after the results were known.
H3 and H4 are exploratory, and the reference points used
later (for example 6 times LRU) are descriptive, not
preregistered acceptance criteria. The paired comparisons
of Table VII, with Holm correction over ten tests, are the
primary inferential analysis; ablation, sensitivity and
sampling-validity results are exploratory. Fig. 2

B. Simulator, Workloads and Baselines
We implemented a single-threaded trace-driven simulator
in C++ with a capacity of C = 1,000 frames, excluding the
first 20,000 references (cold fill) from reported miss ratios.
All policies see identical traces. Policies were sanitychecked against known behaviour: LRU misses on every
reference of a loop larger than memory, MRU matches the
offline optimum on that loop, and the offline optimum is
never exceeded. Workloads (Table III) are generated, not
collected: five access primitives with disjoint page ranges,
combined into stable, abrupt, gradual and recurring
scenarios with known change points, plus composite traces
from five kernel-level access models (column-wise matrix
traversal, hash join, B-tree lookup, external-sort merge and
skewed key-value lookup with scan bursts). Traces are 110
per round: 30 stable, 20 abrupt, 20 gradual, 20 recurring
and 20 composite.

C. Controller Under Test
The controller (Fig. 1) keeps a fast path that serves every
reference with the active policy and updates two counters
per page (last access and an aged count); a sampled subset
of references, selected by a 64-bit hash of the page id with
probability p, feeds reuse-time and window buffers. All
other work runs once per epoch of W references. The
candidate set is {LRU, LFU-A, 2Q, MRU}; the initial
policy is LRU, and after the first baseline period one
shadow evaluation selects a start policy.
Stage 1. The epoch miss ratio mt is standardized as zt = (mt
− μ₀)/σ₀, with μ₀ and σ₀ estimated over a baseline of B = 8
epochs (σ₀ floored at 0.02). A two-sided CUSUM
accumulates
S+t = max(0, S+t−1 + zt − δ) (1)
S−t = max(0, S−t−1 − zt − δ) (2)
and raises an alarm when either statistic exceeds h. Stage
2. On an alarm, the sampled reuse times of the last three
epochs (measured in sampled references, with first-time
references given a large sentinel value) are compared with
the baseline reuse times by a two-sample KS test; drift is
confirmed if p < α with at least 100 samples per side,
otherwise the alarm is rejected. Because reuse time
depends on the workload and not on the active policy, this
stage is intended to separate workload change from policyinduced miss-ratio change.
Shadow evaluation. Ten epochs after confirmation (to
accumulate post-change data), each candidate is simulated
at capacity Cs = round(pC) on the sampled window starting
at the confirmation epoch. The first 40% of the window
warms the shadow caches, and the remainder is split into
four blocks. For candidate π, Δπ is the per-block miss
reduction relative to the active policy’s shadow, Braw the
raw references per block, H a fixed horizon and K = 0.1C a
transition-cost margin in miss-equivalents. The controller
switches to the candidate with the largest margin if
(LCB(Δπ) / Braw) H − K > 0 (3)
where LCB is a one-sided lower confidence bound (z =
1.28) across the four blocks. A reverse switch to the
previous policy within 30 epochs must additionally exceed
K (hysteresis), and a dwell time of 10 epochs applies after
any switch. Switching rebuilds the target policy’s metadata
from the resident pages ordered by last access, after
discarding pages not referenced in the last 20,000
references (A1); LFU-A and 2Q use the aged counts to
place pages. After a confirmed change the controller rebaselines for 4 + 10 epochs plus B epochs. Table V lists all
parameters. The persistence safeguard of the design

D. Metrics and Statistical Analysis
The primary metric is the miss ratio after warm-up,
reported with the percentage reduction relative to LRU.
Detection is evaluated on abrupt, recurring and composite
traces: a boundary is detected if a confirmed alarm occurs
within 30,000 references after it, and the delay is the
distance to that alarm. False confirmations are counted on
stable traces after reference 100,000, which excludes an
initial transient. A reversal is a switch A→B→A within
60,000 references. Cost is the simulated wall-clock time
per reference (minimum of five repetitions on eight traces
on a shared single-core virtual machine; indicative, not a
kernel measurement). Sampling validity (H4) compares the
sampled shadow ranking of the four candidates with a full-

6

capacity simulation of the same raw window. Recovery
time and memory overhead were not measured.
The unit of analysis is the trace (80 non-stable, 30 stable).
Paired differences use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [33],
with Holm correction [35] across the ten baseline
comparisons. Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap
intervals over traces (10,000 resamples for means, 5,000
for paired differences). Effect sizes are mean and median
paired differences with win/loss counts. Traces in one
scenario share a generator, so they are not fully
independent draws from a population of real workloads.
Only five access primitives and five kernel models
underlie all traces, so the effective number of independent
units is smaller than 80; a scenario-stratified or cluster
bootstrap would widen the intervals and is a required
follow-up analysis.

E. Replacement Quality
Table VI and Figs. 3 and 4 give the main result. On the 80
non-stable traces the controller’s mean miss ratio was
0.410 (95% CI 0.387–0.433), 14.2% below LRU (0.477).
The offline optimum reached 0.223, so every online
method leaves a large gap. The ranking of the other
methods was: LPR-style 0.381, S3-FIFO 0.402,
ContShadow 0.407, DA 0.410, LeCaR 0.421, 2Q 0.431,
ARC 0.449 and LRU 0.477. MRU is dominated except on
loops. On stable traces the controller (0.332) was close to
the best fixed policies (S3-FIFO 0.342, 2Q 0.344), mostly
because of its start-up policy selection.
By scenario, the controller was competitive on abrupt
traces (0.412, level with ContShadow at 0.411 and below
S3-FIFO at 0.458) but clearly weaker on gradual drift
(0.474 versus 0.407 for S3-FIFO and 0.405 for LPR-style).
In the gradual scenario the reuse-time distribution moves
slowly, so the KS confirmation was often not triggered
while the policy mixture was changing; a variant without
that stage reached 0.459 on gradual traces against 0.474 (a
per-scenario value not tabulated in Table VIII). On
composite kernel traces all methods except MRU were
within about 0.05 of each other.

G. Detection, Switching and Cost
The monitor and confirmation stages detected 269 of 280
phase boundaries (96.1%) in abrupt, recurring and
composite traces. The median delay was 1,999 references,
one epoch, with essentially no spread; this should be read
with care, because all boundaries in these traces coincide
with epoch boundaries, which favours detection speed.
Two different false-alarm measures are reported and
should not be conflated. (i) On the 30 stable traces, a false
confirmation is any confirmed alarm after reference
100,000, because no true change exists; none occurred. (ii)
On the 60 abrupt, recurring and composite traces, a
spurious confirmation is a confirmed alarm outside every
30,000-reference boundary window; 11 occurred. Measure
(ii) is counted per trace set, can include within-phase
structure such as sliding windows and mixtures, and is not
a per-reference rate. For (i) the manuscript reports 8.4
million stable references with a rule-of-three upper bound
of 0.36 per million. Verification note: 30 traces of
300,000 references counted after reference 100,000 give
6.0 million references (bound 0.50 per million), so the
counting window must be confirmed against the source.
The controller made 2.12 switches per non-stable trace
(LPR-style 1.84, ContShadow 2.98, DA-noCost 3.94) and
no A→B→A reversals in 187 switches.
Cost is the main drawback. Simulated time per reference
was 10.1 ns for LRU, 9.0 for CLOCK, 13.3 for 2Q, 13.3
for S3-FIFO, 16.5 for LPR-style, 65.1 for DA, 141.4 for
ContShadow and 165.7 for LeCaR (Fig. 7). The controller
thus costs 6.4 times LRU, but 54% less than continuous
shadow evaluation. Shadow simulation accounted for 31%
of its time, confirmation tests 3% and switching 3%; the
remaining 63% is the always-on fast-path work (hash
sampling, per-page counters and periodic count aging),
which the prototype does not optimize. Fig. 7 plots miss
ratio against simulated cost; DA lies between the cheap
heuristics and the continuous-shadow methods in cost but
not in miss ratio. These timings come from an unoptimized
simulator on a shared machine and only indicate relative
magnitude. Under any budget below about 6 times LRU,
H3 would not be satisfied by this implementation.

Against 2Q (-0.0217, adjusted p = 0.288), LeCaR, S3FIFO (+0.0080, adjusted p = 0.281) and ContShadow the
differences were not significant. The LPR-style baseline
had a lower mean miss ratio (mean difference +0.0287,
bootstrap interval excluding zero) although the Wilcoxon
test on the median difference of 0.000 was not significant
(adjusted p = 0.332); the distribution is skewed, because
LPR-style wins by a wide margin on loop-containing
phases and ties elsewhere. In round 1 (design A0) the
controller was significantly worse than S3-FIFO (+0.0135,
p < 0.001), so the amendment narrowed but did not
remove the gap.
F. Ablation
Table VIII removes one component at a time. Removing
recency-filtered migration raised the non-stable miss ratio
from 0.410 to 0.432 (paired Wilcoxon p < 0.001), and
replacing shadow evaluation by a random choice raised it
to 0.497 (p < 0.001), worse than LRU (0.477). Both
mechanisms are therefore essential. Removing the cost
margin did not change the miss ratio (0.405, p = 0.65) but
increased switches from 1.70 to 3.05 per trace, so the
margin halves switching at no measurable penalty. The
dwell time was never binding because the evaluation delay
already exceeds it. Removing the KS confirmation lowered
the miss ratio slightly (0.406 versus 0.410, p = 0.12),
which means that, in this data, the second stage did not
improve replacement quality; since false alarms were zero
with or without it on stable traces, we cannot show a
benefit of that stage. ContShadow reached a miss ratio of
0.407, statistically indistinguishable from DA (p = 0.70),
but needed 23.9 shadow evaluations per trace against 4.5
for DA, an 81% reduction attributable to drift gating.

Four lessons emerge. First, the state of the replacement
policy is as important as the choice of policy: switching to
a policy that pins old pages, without first discarding stale
pages, can be catastrophic, and a shadow window that
includes pre-change references misjudges such policies
(the two mechanisms behind amendment A1; the migration
filter alone is worth 0.022 in miss ratio). Second,
evaluating candidates before switching is indispensable
(random choice is no better than LRU), but it need not be
continuous: gating it on confirmed drift removed 81% of
the evaluations at equal miss ratio. Third, a cost margin on
switching reduces switching by about half without a
measurable loss. Fourth, a simple regret-driven LRU/MRU
heuristic beat the full controller on average, mostly
because loops larger than memory are common in the test
workloads and MRU solves them after a short LRU flush;

VII. LIMITATIONS AND SCOPE OF CLAIMS
This paper evaluates one controller design in a simulator.
It does not establish that the controller outperforms the
best existing replacement algorithms: S3-FIFO and a
simple LPR-style heuristic had lower mean miss ratios,
and the difference to LPR-style is not removed by the
amendment. The comparison omits several strong
published methods (CAR, LIRS, CACHEUS, 3L-Cache,
S4-FIFO,
EACLOCK
and
Clock2Q+)
because
implementations were unavailable, so the ranking among
methods applies only to the compared set. The candidate
set of the controller is small and excludes ARC and S3FIFO; including them could change the results. The
implemented design omits the persistence safeguard and
uses only two monitored features. Recovery time and
memory overhead were not measured, and the controller
performs poorly on gradual drift in this configuration.
The literature review is narrative, not systematic, and its
limitations in Table I are partly inferred. The study makes
no claim about production kernels, real application traces,
or latency. We claim only (i) a characterization of driftgated policy selection in a controlled setting, (ii) evidence
that state migration and shadow evaluation are essential
components, (iii) evidence that drift gating can reduce
shadow-evaluation work by 81% at equal miss ratio, and
(iv) a reproducible protocol, including its amendment
history, for testing such controllers.

VI. THREATS TO VALIDITY
Construct validity. The traces are generated: five synthetic
access primitives and five kernel-level access models, not
traces recorded from running systems. Generated phases
have clean boundaries aligned with the controller’s epochs,
which favours detection delay, and workload types such as
loops larger than memory are overrepresented relative to
their frequency in practice, which favours policies such as
MRU and regret-driven LRU/MRU switching. Miss ratio
is also partly determined by the active policy, so the Stage1 monitor can mistake a policy-induced change for
workload change; the reuse-time confirmation stage was
designed to address this, but our data did not show a
measurable benefit from it.
Internal validity. The design was amended once after the
first test round, using information from that round. We
mitigated this by validating the amendment on training
traces only and evaluating on a fresh trace set, by keeping
all parameters fixed, and by reporting both rounds; a
reader should nevertheless treat the amended design as
having had one additional degree of freedom. Reimplementations of ARC, S3-FIFO, 2Q, LeCaR and the
LPR-style heuristic may differ from the original
implementations, and baselines were not tuned per
workload, which can favour or penalize them. The
simulator was sanity-checked against known behaviour but
not against reference implementations. Seeds were fixed
and the shadow simulator’s sampling is deterministic.
External validity. A single-threaded trace-driven simulator
omits concurrency, memory pressure from multiple
processes, reclaim interactions, writeback, I/O queueing
and NUMA effects. Cost figures come from an
unoptimized simulator on a shared virtual machine and
should be read as relative magnitudes. Results at one
nominal capacity (with sensitivity at two others) and one
trace length may not transfer to other scales. Findings are
limited to the evaluated workload families.
Statistical conclusion validity. The switching rule applies a
normal quantile (z = 1.28) to only four autocorrelated
shadow blocks; a t-quantile with three degrees of freedom
is about 1.64, so the nominal 90% bound is optimistic. KS
p-values on dependent, tied reuse times are not calibrated
error rates, so the confirmation stage provides a heuristic
gate rather than formal error control. Traces within a
scenario share a generator, so the effective number of
independent workloads is smaller than 80, and the stated pvalues and intervals describe variability over generated
traces, not over real workloads. Wilcoxon tests with ten
corrected comparisons have limited power for differences
of about 0.01 in mean miss ratio. The H3 budget and H4
threshold were not pre-registered. Several secondary
results (for example the KS stage having no measurable
effect) are non-significant differences and should not be
read as evidence of no effect.

VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper reviewed 15 studies from 2025–2026 on
adaptive replacement, statistical drift detection and
emerging memory systems, and found that the three areas
are weakly connected. To test whether closing the gap
pays off, we implemented a drift-gated controller with
CUSUM monitoring, KS confirmation, sampled shadow
simulation and a cost-aware switching rule, and evaluated
it against eleven baselines (ten in the paired tests) and an
offline optimum on 110 held-out generated traces after
freezing parameters on training data and re-testing on fresh
traces.
The controller reduced the mean miss ratio on non-stable
traces by 14.2% relative to LRU and was significantly
better than LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC. It was
not better than S3-FIFO or an LPR-style LRU/MRU
heuristic, and it cost about 6.4 times LRU per reference in
the simulator. Detection was fast and clean on abrupt
phase changes, and drift gating reduced shadow-evaluation
work by 81% relative to continuous evaluation at the same
miss ratio. The ablations identify recency-filtered state
migration and shadow evaluation as essential, and show
that the second-stage confirmation did not help here.
Statistically gated policy selection is therefore a viable but
not yet superior alternative to existing adaptive
replacement.
Future work should (i) repeat the evaluation on recorded
traces from real systems (for example the MSR Cambridge
block traces [31], noting that storage-block traces are
cache-replacement
evidence,
not
virtual-memory
evidence), (ii) include the missing baselines and a wider
candidate set that contains ARC and S3-FIFO, (iii)
combine drift gating with regret feedback to handle
gradual change, (iv) reduce always-on overhead and

latency, and (v) pre-register overhead budgets and
agreement thresholds before testing.
