\noindent{\small\bfseries\itshape Abstract---}{\small\bfseries
Drift-triggered policy switching is often proposed to make adaptive page replacement cheaper, but it is unclear when the saving is worth any loss in accuracy. We report a reproducible measurement study of a controller that combines a CUSUM drift trigger, sampled shadow evaluation of candidate policies, and a cost-margin switching rule. A Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) confirmation stage had only a small ablation effect and is not treated as a validated gate. On 80 non-stable generated traces, gating cut shadow evaluations by 76\% relative to continuous evaluation (6.47 vs.\ 27.39 per trace) but raised the mean miss ratio (0.453 vs.\ 0.423), and it was not significantly different from S3-FIFO (0.468; Holm-adjusted $p=0.09$). On six public FIU block traces the controller never switched, because the shadow cache was larger than the sampled working set and could not rank policies ($\Cs/\Dw\ge 1$); a later post hoc check confirmed a sharp rankability boundary at this ratio. At matched evaluation budgets CUSUM beat periodic and random triggers, while an oracle trigger and continuous evaluation exposed an accuracy ceiling that Page--Hinkley and simplified ADWIN detectors did not close. A user-space minor-fault calibration places the DA--ContShadow crossover near 2.2\us{} (95\% CI 1.44--4.06\us), but total-time accounting that includes CLOCK and S3-FIFO shows DA is never the cheapest policy on the measured means: CLOCK is cheapest below 0.22\us, S3-FIFO between 0.22 and 2.30\us, and continuous evaluation above 2.30\us, with DA at least 3.2\,ns per reference above the best alternative at every miss cost. DA is never best in these measurements: gating reduces shadow work but is not the cheapest total-time choice.\par}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Physical memory is finite. When a process references a page that is not resident and no free frame is available, the memory manager must evict a resident page. The rule that makes this choice, the page-replacement policy, affects page-fault frequency, storage traffic, tail latency and application throughput. Recency-based policies such as least recently used (LRU) and its CLOCK approximation remain the practical default in operating systems, while the research literature offers frequency-aware, scan-resistant and self-tuning alternatives, including ARC~\cite{arc}, CAR~\cite{car}, LIRS~\cite{lirs}, 2Q~\cite{twoq} and S3-FIFO~\cite{s3fifo}.

No single policy is best for every access pattern. A sequential scan can flush a cache tuned for repeated reuse, a loop slightly larger than memory makes LRU miss on every reference, and a policy that protects a previously hot working set can retain pages that are useless after a phase change. Real workloads move between such regimes: a database alternates between index lookups and table scans, and a multitasking system between applications. The question studied here is therefore not which policy is best, but whether a system can recognize that reference behavior has changed and respond at an acceptable cost.

The stakes have grown. Containerized multi-tenant servers interleave unrelated workloads on shared memory, accelerator memory is oversubscribed by multitasking and large-model serving, and tiered memory makes a wrong eviction more expensive~\cite{msched,gpuext,prefix}. Recent work shows that adaptive and learned policies can reduce misses substantially~\cite{l3cache,clock2q,s4fifo}, but also that LRU remains competitive in some settings~\cite{prefix} and that policy rankings depend on the workload~\cite{stale}. A principled account of when adaptation pays for itself is therefore needed, not only another adaptive algorithm.

We use \emph{workload drift} to mean a statistically meaningful change over time in the distribution or temporal structure of page references. Two research lines address related problems. Adaptive replacement algorithms infer from feedback which behavior to favor: ARC shifts capacity between recency and frequency lists, LeCaR and CACHEUS reweight expert policies by regret, and recent learned designs reduce the cost of learning on the eviction path~\cite{lecar,cacheus,l3cache,s4fifo}. Concept-drift research instead provides formal change detectors with controllable error rates, such as adaptive windowing and sequential tests~\cite{gama,adwin,pudd,spc,dynthr,adwinu}. A detector built for a machine-learning pipeline may be too expensive, or may rely on labels or prediction errors that a page-reference stream does not have; a continuously adapting replacement algorithm, conversely, may switch behavior without any control over the rate of unnecessary switches. The closest precedent outside paging is Acme~\cite{acme}, which keeps a shadow cache of the alternative query-caching mode and switches when a significance test favors it. Statistically guided cache adaptation is therefore not new. What remains open is whether drift-gated selection among page-replacement policies is effective once the costs of monitoring, shadow evaluation and policy-state migration are counted, and how it compares with strong existing algorithms.

Four constraints make drift-aware replacement hard. (a)~\emph{Rate:} references arrive at memory speed, so per-reference statistical work is prohibitive and monitoring must be sampled or summarized by epoch. (b)~\emph{Missing labels and policy-dependent signals:} there is no ground truth for ``the workload changed,'' and the miss ratio depends on the active policy, so a switch can itself create an apparent change. (c)~\emph{State:} policies keep different metadata, and a switch must rebuild it from the resident pages. (d)~\emph{Error control:} false alarms waste work and cause oscillation, while missed changes leave a mismatched policy in place.

\textbf{Research questions.}
\textbf{RQ1:} How does a drift-gated controller with sampled shadow evaluation compare, in miss ratio, with continuous shadow evaluation and with strong static policies under controlled phase changes?
\textbf{RQ2:} How much shadow work does gating save, and at what miss cost does the saving outweigh any loss in accuracy?
\textbf{RQ3:} Do the findings hold on external traces, and what limits shadow evaluation there?

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled measurement of drift gating against continuous shadow evaluation and strong static baselines, on freshly generated phase traces, six public block traces, and instrumented real programs with whole programs held out. We report the null and negative outcomes.
\item A cost break-even model that turns the accuracy--cost trade-off into a condition on miss cost, calibrated with a measured user-space minor-fault latency, plus a total-time envelope analysis showing that DA is never the cheapest policy in these measurements (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\item A rankability diagnostic ($\Cs/\Dw$) that explains why sampled shadow evaluation fails on the public traces, and a fully reproducible negative-result protocol: released simulator, seeds, configurations, per-trace CSVs, selection sweep, ablations, and scripts that regenerate every table and figure.
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim a new replacement algorithm or a validated statistical gate; the effective mechanism is a CUSUM trigger with sampled shadow evaluation.

\textbf{Summary of findings.} On 80 fresh non-stable traces the gated controller (DA) reduced the mean miss ratio by 20.5\% relative to LRU, but was not significantly different from S3-FIFO and was significantly worse than continuous shadow evaluation (ContShadow) by 0.030 miss ratio, while saving 76\% of shadow evaluations. On six public block traces the frozen controller never switched and was Pareto-dominated by S3-FIFO. On 15 instrumented programs with whole programs held out it tied S3-FIFO at higher cost, and no comparison survived Holm correction. In total-time terms DA was never the cheapest: CLOCK, S3-FIFO and ContShadow each occupy part of the lower envelope, and DA remained at least 3.2\,ns/reference above the best alternative at every miss cost. The DA--ContShadow crossover is about 2.2\us, but S3-FIFO is cheaper than DA below 2.52\us{} and CLOCK below 0.22\us.

\textbf{Scope.} The main experiments use generated traces, not traces collected from running systems. The public traces are storage/cache traces, not virtual-memory paging traces, and serve only as an external check. A faithful kernel multi-generation LRU (MGLRU) baseline is not possible in page-level trace replay. A nine-boot QEMU guest observation later measured real in-kernel fault-path durations, but reached no reclaim function and built no in-kernel DA policy. The simulator measures algorithmic behavior and relative cost, not kernel performance. Sections~\ref{sec:threats},~\ref{sec:pdl_async} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} state these limits in detail.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} gives background and related work, Section~\ref{sec:method} the controller and protocol, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, Section~\ref{sec:cost} the cost model, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} a synthesis of when gating pays off, and Section~\ref{sec:threats} threats to validity and limitations.

\subsection{Adaptive replacement and drift detection in context}
Prior work spans adaptive replacement~\cite{seq,eelru,arc,car,lirs,twoq,clockpro,s3fifo,acme02,lecar,cacheus,l3cache,lpr,eaclock,clock2q,s4fifo}, explicit change detectors~\cite{gama,adwin,page,pudd,spc,dynthr,tae,htm,adwinu}, and sampled or shadow-based policy evaluation~\cite{mini,shards,acme}; system studies cover GPU multitasking and tiered caches~\cite{msched,gpuext,prefix,stale}. The supplementary literature table (Table~\ref{S-tab:related}) maps the closest prior work to this study. Four gaps follow: generic detectors are rarely evaluated on page-reference feature streams at memory-management rates (Gap~1, inferred from the cited scopes), statistical change confirmation is not compared with heuristic adaptation in a controlled experiment (Gap~2), transition costs are not reported together with accuracy (Gap~3), and studies are not comparable across workloads and metrics (Gap~4). This is a focused narrative review rather than a systematic one; the full discussion, scope and caveats are in the supplement.

\section{Controller and Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Reproducibility.} Every experimental number is produced by released code in the accompanying package: the trace generator, all baselines, the controller, seeds and raw per-trace output, the parameter-selection sweep (\code{sweep_selection.py}), the primary-study driver (\code{run_main_study.sh}), the table builder (\code{analyze_main_study.py}), the appendix interval script (\code{appendix_intervals.py}), the cost model (\code{cost_model.py}), the public-trace converter (\code{convert_public_traces.py}) and the fault-latency microbenchmark (\code{prototype_fault_latency.cpp}).

\subsection{Simulator, Workloads and Baselines}
We implemented a single-threaded trace-driven simulator in C++ with capacity $C=1{,}000$ frames, excluding the first 20{,}000 references (cold fill) from reported miss ratios. All policies see identical traces; sanity checks include LRU missing every reference of a loop larger than memory and MRU matching the offline optimum on that loop. Generated workloads combine five access primitives into stable, abrupt, gradual, recurring and composite scenarios (110 traces per round: 30 stable and 20 per non-stable scenario); full workload definitions are in Table~\ref{S-tab:workloads} of the supplement.

\textbf{Methods compared.} We evaluate fixed policies (LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A, MRU, 2Q, ARC, S3-FIFO), adaptive comparators (LeCaR, an LPR-style re-implementation, and ContShadow, an Acme-style control without a drift gate), the proposed DA controller, five ablations, and OPT as a reference. \subsection{Controller Under Test}
A fast path serves every reference with the active policy; hash-based sampling (probability $p$) feeds reuse-time and window buffers, and epoch-level work runs every $W$ references. A two-sided CUSUM on the standardized epoch miss ratio triggers a KS confirmation on sampled reuse times; ten epochs later each candidate is simulated on a sampled shadow cache, and a lower-confidence-bound margin rule (frozen margin $K=0$) selects a switch after a dwell period. The candidate set \{LRU, LFU-A, 2Q, MRU, S3-FIFO\} was selected on training traces, so S3-FIFO is inside DA's candidate set and DA is nevertheless not significantly better than it. The architecture (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:arch}), equations, all frozen parameters (Table~\ref{S-tab:params}) and the KS/switching details are in the supplement.

\subsection{Selection Protocol and Evaluation History}
\label{sec:protocol}
The configuration was selected by a 48-configuration sweep on 47 training traces (seed 100) and frozen for the fresh test traces (seed 5001; 30 stable and 80 non-stable); no parameter was changed afterwards. One earlier round (seeds 1001+) informed amendment A1 before the single fresh evaluation, so these results reflect one round of test feedback. The full sweep, training grid and non-retained Round-1 history are in the supplement (Section~S2.3, Fig.~\ref{S-fig:workflow}).

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
The primary metric is post-warm-up miss ratio. Detection uses 380 true boundaries over the 80 non-stable traces (alarm within 30{,}000 references); uncertainty is a 10{,}000-resample percentile interval over traces, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Holm correction across ten baseline comparisons. Clustered resampling has only 35 distinct resamples and is a sensitivity note, not the headline interval. Full definitions, activations and caveats are in the supplement.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All numbers in Sections~\ref{sec:quality}--\ref{sec:costsim} come from the frozen configuration and the fresh test traces; raw per-trace CSVs are in the reproduction package.

\subsection{Replacement Quality}
\label{sec:quality}
On the 80 non-stable traces the controller's mean miss ratio was 0.453 (95\% CI 0.423--0.484), 20.5\% below LRU (0.570); the offline optimum reached 0.302 (Table~\ref{tab:miss}; per-trace plots in Figs.~\ref{S-fig:overall} and~\ref{S-fig:scen} of the supplement). DA was better than S3-FIFO (0.468) on the mean, but the paired difference was not significant (Holm-adjusted $p=0.09$; supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:paired}). It was significantly worse than continuous shadow evaluation (ContShadow, 0.423) by 0.030 miss ratio (adjusted $p<0.001$). DA was significantly better than eight of the ten paired baselines (all adjusted $p<0.001$). The exceptions are S3-FIFO, where the evidence for an improvement over the strongest fixed policy is weak, and ContShadow, where the difference is unfavorable: continuous evaluation was more accurate than drift-gated evaluation in this study.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Removing shadow evaluation raised the non-stable miss ratio from 0.453 to 0.619, worse than LRU; all other ablations scored 0.453--0.456 (supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:ablation}). The KS stage fired on 643 alarms and rejected 173, but changed the miss ratio little; the cost margin and dwell rule were inactive under $K=0$ and cannot be evaluated from this ablation. The recency filter acted at 47 of 213 switches. Full itemized diagnostics are in the supplement. S3-FIFO costs less than DA (28.5 vs.\ 67.4\,ns per reference) but is less accurate, whereas ContShadow is both more accurate and more expensive; gating saved 76.4\% of shadow evaluations (6.47 vs.\ 27.39 per trace) without matching ContShadow's accuracy.

\section{Cost Break-Even and Total-Time Envelope}
\label{sec:cost}
The measured trade-off is a 0.030 miss-ratio penalty for DA against a 66.0\,ns per reference time saving and 20.9 fewer shadow evaluations per trace. Let $c_m$ be the cost of one miss and $c_e$ the cost of one shadow evaluation. With the same fast path, total time per trace is
\begin{equation}
T=N t_0+N m\,c_m+E\,c_e,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is trace length, $m$ the miss ratio, $E$ shadow evaluations and $t_0$ the per-reference cost excluding shadow work. DA beats ContShadow iff
\begin{equation}
(m_{\mathrm{DA}}-m_{\mathrm{CS}})\,c_m+(E_{\mathrm{DA}}-E_{\mathrm{CS}})\,\frac{c_e}{N}<0,
\end{equation}
i.e. $c_e>R\,c_m$ with $R=N(m_{\mathrm{DA}}-m_{\mathrm{CS}})/(E_{\mathrm{CS}}-E_{\mathrm{DA}})$, so the crossover is
\begin{equation}
c_m^{*}=c_e/R.
\label{eq:breakeven}
\end{equation}
The frozen means give $R=783.28$ (95\% CI [426, 1187]) and a measured-time crossover of about 2.2\us{} (95\% CI 1.44--4.06\us); a direct timing build shifts it to about 3.1\us, and the user-space minor-fault calibration is 1.98--2.06\us. DA is never the cheapest policy on the measured means: CLOCK is cheapest below 0.216\us, S3-FIFO between 0.216 and 2.305\us, and ContShadow above 2.305\us. DA is never on the envelope, with a minimum excess of 3.27\,ns/reference at 2.305\us (bootstrap minimum-gap interval [$-28.6,40.2$]\,ns). At the measured minor-fault cost S3-FIFO is cheapest. The full derivation, direct-cost check, calibration discussion and bootstrap intervals are in the supplement.

\section{Discussion: When Does Gating Pay Off?}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Hypotheses.} H1 (DA beats S3-FIFO in miss ratio) and H2 (a two-stage gate improves on CUSUM alone) are not supported, and H3 (competitive per-reference cost) is not met: ContShadow was more accurate, KS changed the miss ratio little (0.456 without vs.\ 0.453 with), and DA is 2.7$\times$ LRU per reference. \textbf{When gating pays off.} Taken together, the measurements identify where gating saves shadow work and where it sits in total time.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Gating is never the cheapest option in these measurements.} On the measured means, CLOCK is cheapest below 0.22\us, S3-FIFO between 0.22 and 2.30\us, and ContShadow above 2.30\us. DA beats ContShadow only below 2.20\us, but S3-FIFO is cheaper than DA below 2.52\us, so no miss-cost regime makes gating the best total-time choice. At the measured minor-fault cost, S3-FIFO is cheapest and DA is about 5\,ns/reference above it.
\item \emph{Drift is abrupt or recurring.} CUSUM detected 97\% of abrupt and 96\% of recurring boundaries, but only 24\% of gradual ones, where it also produced most of the false alarms.
\item \emph{Shadow caches are rankable.} Sampled shadow evaluation can rank policies only when $\Cs/\Dw<1$; otherwise all candidates tie on compulsory misses and the controller never switches.
\item \emph{Accuracy is not the benefit.} Continuous evaluation was more accurate (0.423 vs.\ 0.453), and an oracle trigger was more accurate still (0.4335), so gating is a cost-saving mechanism, not an accuracy mechanism, in these measurements.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Design lessons.} Shadow evaluation is the component that matters (without it the controller is worse than LRU); CUSUM with sampled shadows cut shadow work by 76\% at a 0.030 miss-ratio cost; the KS stage, cost margin and dwell rule were inactive or small-effect; and on 15 programs DA tied ContShadow but not S3-FIFO. The effective mechanism is a CUSUM trigger with sampled shadow evaluation, not a validated two-stage gate.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Construct validity.} Generated phases have clean epoch-aligned boundaries and overrepresent loops larger than memory, so the 73.2\% detection rate and 2{,}000-reference delay are conditional and likely optimistic; miss ratio also depends on the active policy, and KS (active on 643 alarms) had only a small effect.

\textbf{Internal validity.} Training-only selection was followed by one round of test feedback, the FIU capacity was selected on an independent pilot trace, and several baselines are re-implementations that may differ from reference code; seeds and sampling are deterministic.

\textbf{External validity.} A single-threaded simulator omits concurrency, reclaim, writeback, I/O queueing and NUMA effects; cost figures are from an unoptimized simulator. Results at one capacity and trace length may not transfer, public traces are storage traces rather than paging traces, and only 15 program-level clusters are available.

\textbf{Statistical conclusion validity.} Four autocorrelated blocks with a normal LCB ($z=1.28$) are optimistic; a $t$-quantile gave DA 0.4586 [0.4282,0.4888] vs.\ 0.4529 [0.4224,0.4834]. KS $p$-values are not calibrated error rates, clustered traces limit power, and small non-significant differences should not be read as no effect. Full threats and scope-of-claims discussion is in the supplement.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper asked when drift gating pays off in page replacement and answered it as a reproducible measurement study. A CUSUM-triggered controller with sampled shadow evaluation saved 76\% of shadow evaluations relative to continuous evaluation on 80 non-stable generated traces, but had a higher miss ratio (0.453 vs.\ 0.423) and was not significantly different from S3-FIFO (Holm-adjusted $p=0.09$). On public FIU traces it never switched, because the shadow cache exceeded the sampled working set; follow-ups located a sharp rankability boundary at $\Cs/\Dw=1$ without a meaningful repair gain. CUSUM beat periodic and random triggers at matched budgets but not an oracle trigger or continuous evaluation, and Page--Hinkley and simplified ADWIN did not close the gap. The cost model, calibrated by a user-space minor-fault measurement, gives a DA--ContShadow crossover at about 2.2--3.1\us, but a total-time comparison that includes CLOCK and S3-FIFO shows DA is never the cheapest policy on the measured means: CLOCK, S3-FIFO and ContShadow each occupy part of the lower envelope, and DA remains at least 3.2\,ns/reference above the best alternative at every miss cost. The measurements therefore do not identify a regime in which drift gating pays off in total time; DA is never best in these measurements, and they identify only a regime in which it is cheaper than continuous evaluation. A nine-boot QEMU guest observation produced real fault-path durations but no reclaim event, no controller toggle and no kernel DA policy; stronger claims require additional production traces and kernel-level replacement measurements.
