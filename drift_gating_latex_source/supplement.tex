\section{Background and Related Work: Full Narrative}
\label{sec:supp_related}

\subsection{Page Replacement and Workload Phases}
A reference trace is an ordered sequence of page identifiers. For a capacity of $C$ frames, a replacement policy chooses a victim whenever a reference misses and all frames are occupied. LRU evicts the least recently used page, LFU the least frequently used, FIFO the oldest, CLOCK approximates LRU with a reference bit, and MRU evicts the most recently used page, which is favorable for cyclic scans larger than memory. Hybrid designs use several queues to separate one-time from repeated references~\cite{arc,car,lirs,twoq,s3fifo}. The miss ratio is the fraction of references that miss; it is necessary but incomplete as a quality measure, because metadata cost, decision cost and adaptation speed also matter. Results on storage-block traces are cache-replacement results, not direct evidence about virtual-memory paging.

Phases differ in working-set size, reuse-distance distribution, sequentiality and frequency skew. Drift may be abrupt, gradual or recurring~\cite{gama}. Phase changes also interact with policy state: a policy that retains pages of the previous phase (for example MRU, which pins the oldest resident pages) can perform far worse than its steady-state behavior suggests until that state is flushed. This interaction is central to the design and results of this paper.

\subsection{Adaptive Replacement}
SEQ~\cite{seq} detects long fault sequences and handles them separately, and EELRU~\cite{eelru} adjusts its eviction point from recency-distribution evidence. ARC balances recency and frequency using ghost-list hits, CAR transfers the idea to CLOCK, LIRS uses inter-reference recency, 2Q separates newly admitted from re-referenced pages, and CLOCK-Pro~\cite{clockpro} adapts a CLOCK variant to reuse distance. S3-FIFO shows that FIFO queues with a small admission queue can match or beat more complex designs. ACME~\cite{acme02}, LeCaR and CACHEUS mix expert policies using regret or learned feedback. Recent designs reduce the cost of learning: 3L-Cache~\cite{l3cache} moves learning and inference off the critical path (evaluated on 4{,}855 CDN, key-value and block traces, with overhead still above LRU), S4-FIFO~\cite{s4fifo} learns configuration parameters of a FIFO-family heuristic rather than every eviction, LPR~\cite{lpr} uses re-access feedback to switch among policies such as LRU and MRU with a heuristic rather than statistical signal, EACLOCK~\cite{eaclock} adapts a CLOCK-style policy from hit-rate feedback, and Clock2Q+~\cite{clock2q} targets correlated metadata references in VMware vSAN, reporting up to 28.5\% lower miss ratio than S3-FIFO on metadata traces. Newer eviction baselines used in our follow-ups include SIEVE~\cite{sieve}, W-TinyLFU~\cite{wtinylfu} and LHD~\cite{lhd}.

Two further lines of prior art are directly relevant. Set dueling, introduced for dynamic insertion policies (DIP/DRRIP)~\cite{dip}, dedicates sampler sets to two candidate policies and lets the one with fewer sampler misses control the whole cache at very low storage cost. The multi-generation LRU (MGLRU) reclaim path of Linux~\cite{mglru} groups pages into generations by access recency and ages them incrementally. Both are sampled or hierarchical ways of adapting recency treatment and are natural comparators for a drift-gated controller. Whether their mechanisms transfer to drift-gated page replacement is our inference: none of the cited evaluations measures shadow-evaluation cost, policy-state migration and post-switch warm-up together on page-reference streams.

\subsection{Drift Detection}
Concept-drift research distinguishes changes in the input distribution, in the input--output relationship, or both~\cite{gama}. A page-reference stream has no labels, so a controller must monitor observable summaries such as the miss ratio or the reuse-time distribution. ADWIN tests whether sub-windows have different means~\cite{adwin}; Page--Hinkley and CUSUM procedures accumulate deviations from a reference level and signal when the sum exceeds a threshold~\cite{page}; two-sample tests such as Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) compare distributions. Each has assumptions about sample size, independence and repeated testing. Recent detectors include prediction-uncertainty drift detection (PUDD), which needs a predictive model~\cite{pudd}; a label-free statistical-process-control detector evaluated by simulation of model-monitoring scenarios~\cite{spc}; dynamic threshold determination, which maintains parallel candidate models~\cite{dynthr}; transformer--autoencoder and HTM-plus-SPRT frameworks that impose training or univariate-data assumptions~\cite{tae,htm}; and ADWIN-U for unsupervised settings~\cite{adwinu}. None is evaluated on page-reference streams or at memory-management rates.

\subsection{Sampling and Shadow Evaluation}
Miniature simulation~\cite{mini} emulates a larger cache with a smaller one, and SHARDS~\cite{shards} estimates miss-ratio curves from spatially hashed reuse-distance samples. Both make on-demand shadow evaluation feasible, although sampling can distort temporal structure and policy rankings. Section~S3.5 of this supplement shows a concrete failure of this kind. Acme~\cite{acme} simulates the alternative caching mode through shadow entries and switches when a one-tailed $t$-test at the 0.05 level favors it, reporting execution time within 16\% of the optimal mode in the worst case and 4\% on average on TPC-H workloads.

\subsection{GPU, Multi-Tier and Benchmark Studies}
MSched~\cite{msched} predicts the next task's working set from kernel launch arguments to migrate GPU memory proactively, which relies on a predictability that CPU page streams lack. gpu\_ext~\cite{gpuext} makes GPU-driver policies replaceable eBPF programs. A study of LLM prefix caches finds LRU surprisingly competitive~\cite{prefix}, and a stale-tolerant cache benchmark finds the ranking workload-dependent across four synthetic patterns, with ARC highest on average~\cite{stale}.

\subsection{Research Gaps}
Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes the closest prior work. The reviewed work establishes that adaptive replacement is well studied, that learning-based policies can respond online to access patterns, that statistical drift detection offers explicit change evidence, and that cache-mode selection has used shadow evaluation with significance testing~\cite{acme}. Four gaps follow. \textbf{Gap~1 (detectors on reference streams):} generic drift detectors are rarely evaluated on page-reference feature streams at memory-management rates and overhead budgets. \textbf{Gap~2 (statistical gating vs.\ heuristic adaptation):} no reviewed study compares explicit statistical change confirmation against heuristics such as LPR in a controlled experiment. \textbf{Gap~3 (transition costs):} detector execution, shadow simulation, policy-state preparation and post-switch warm-up are not consistently reported together with miss ratio and detection delay. \textbf{Gap~4 (comparability):} studies use different workloads, metrics and system contexts, so their results cannot be compared as if from a shared benchmark. Gaps~2--4 are direct observations about the reviewed set; Gap~1 is inferred from the stated scopes of the cited detectors, not a claim that no such evaluation exists elsewhere.

\emph{Scope of the review.} This is a focused narrative review, not a systematic one. The authors selected 15 peer-reviewed publications and preprints from 2025--2026 plus the foundational work needed to explain the lineage. No preregistered search protocol, duplicate screening or quality scoring was applied, several included works are preprints, and one study~\cite{pudd} first appeared as a preprint in December 2024 before AAAI 2025. Reported results are not pooled or normalized, and limitations are framed against the goal of low-overhead drift-aware replacement, not against the original studies' own aims. Each component of the controller studied here has precedent (ADWIN, miniature simulation, SHARDS, statistical cache-mode selection); we make no priority claim for the combination and examine only its measured consequences.

\section{Controller and Experimental Protocol: Full Detail}
\label{sec:supp_method}

We implemented a single-threaded trace-driven simulator in C++ with capacity $C=1{,}000$ frames, excluding the first 20{,}000 references (cold fill) from reported miss ratios. All policies see identical traces. Policies were sanity-checked against known behavior: LRU misses on every reference of a loop larger than memory, and MRU matches the offline optimum on that loop. OPT~\cite{belady} minimizes misses over the whole trace including the cold fill, so it is a reference point rather than a strict lower bound on the post-warm-up miss ratio.

Workloads (Table~\ref{tab:workloads}) are generated, not collected: five access primitives with disjoint page ranges are combined into stable, abrupt, gradual and recurring scenarios with known change points, plus composite traces from five kernel-level access models (column-wise matrix traversal, hash join, B-tree lookup, external-sort merge, and skewed key-value lookup with scan bursts). Each round has 110 traces: 30 stable and 20 each of abrupt, gradual, recurring and composite.

\textbf{Methods compared.} \emph{Fixed:} LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A (counts halved every $4C$ references, scaled to the capacity in use), MRU, 2Q ($K_{\mathrm{in}}=C/4$, $K_{\mathrm{out}}=C/2$), ARC, and S3-FIFO (10\% small queue, ghost queue equal to main size). \emph{Adaptive:} LeCaR (re-implementation), an LPR-style policy (re-implementing the principle of~\cite{lpr}: re-access ``regret'' feedback with probationary LRU/MRU switching, not the authors' code), and ContShadow, our Acme-style control that runs the same shadow evaluation and switching rule every ten epochs without a drift gate. \emph{Proposed:} DA, the drift-gated controller. \emph{Ablations:} DA-noPurge (A0 migration), DA-noS2 (no KS confirmation), DA-noShadow (random pick), DA-noCost (no cost margin) and DA-noDwell. \emph{Reference:} OPT. The main quality table reports eleven non-proposed baselines (eight fixed, three adaptive); the paired table below pairs DA against ten of them, omitting MRU.

The two count-aging periods are intentional: LFU-A halves its frequency counts every $4C$ references (4{,}000 at $C=1{,}000$; every $4\Cs=800$ at $\Cs=200$ inside shadow evaluation), whereas the controller's own fast-path aged count is halved every 50 epochs (100{,}000 references) and is used, together with the last-access time, only to place pages when a switch migrates state. CAR, LIRS, CLOCK-Pro, CACHEUS, 3L-style, DIP and MGLRU-style are present in the released simulator and raw CSV outputs but are omitted from the main quality table to keep the main comparison focused on the central fixed/adaptive baselines and the evaluated controller; their raw results are retained in the reproduction package and their omission does not alter the reported DA-vs-S3-FIFO/ContShadow conclusions. S4-FIFO, EACLOCK and Clock2Q+ were not implemented. Re-implementations follow the cited descriptions and may differ from the original code in important ways.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the controller. A fast path serves every reference with the active policy and updates two counters per page (last access and an aged count). A sampled subset of references, selected by a 64-bit hash of the page id with probability $p$, feeds reuse-time and window buffers. All other work runs once per epoch of $W$ references. The candidate set is \{LRU, LFU-A, 2Q, MRU, S3-FIFO\}, selected on training traces (Section~\ref{sec:supp_protocol}); S3-FIFO is therefore inside DA's candidate set, yet DA is not significantly better than S3-FIFO. The initial policy is LRU, and after the first baseline period one shadow evaluation selects a start policy.

\textbf{Stage 1 (CUSUM).} The epoch miss ratio $m_t$ is standardized as $z_t=(m_t-\mu_0)/\sigma_0$, with $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ estimated over a baseline of $B=8$ epochs ($\sigma_0$ floored at 0.02). A two-sided CUSUM accumulates
\begin{align}
S_t^{+}&=\max\!\bigl(0,\;S_{t-1}^{+}+z_t-\delta\bigr),\\
S_t^{-}&=\max\!\bigl(0,\;S_{t-1}^{-}-z_t-\delta\bigr),
\end{align}
and raises an alarm when either statistic exceeds $h$.

\textbf{Stage 2 (KS confirmation).} On an alarm, the sampled reuse times of the last three epochs (measured in sampled references, with first-time references given a large sentinel value) are compared with the baseline reuse times by a two-sample KS test. Drift is confirmed if $p<\alpha$ with at least 100 samples per side; otherwise the alarm is rejected. Reuse time depends on the workload and not on the active policy, so this stage is intended to separate workload change from policy-induced miss-ratio change.

\textbf{Shadow evaluation and switching.} Ten epochs after confirmation (to accumulate post-change data), each candidate is simulated at capacity $\Cs=\mathrm{round}(pC)$ on the sampled window starting at the confirmation epoch. The first 40\% of the window warms the shadow caches and the remainder is split into four blocks. For candidate $\pi$, let $\Delta_\pi$ be the per-block reduction in sampled misses relative to the active policy's shadow, $B_{\mathrm{raw}}$ the number of raw references per block, $H$ a fixed horizon, and $K$ a transition-cost margin in miss-equivalents (design default $K=0.1C$). Dividing by $p$ rescales sampled misses to raw references. The controller switches to the candidate with the largest margin if
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{LCB}(\Delta_\pi)}{p\,B_{\mathrm{raw}}}\,H-K>0,
\label{eq:switch}
\end{equation}
where LCB is a one-sided lower confidence bound ($z=1.28$) across the four blocks. The frozen configuration sets $K=0$ (selected on training traces), so the margin term did not constrain the reported switches. A reverse switch to the previous policy within 30 epochs must additionally exceed $K$ (hysteresis), and a dwell time of 10 epochs applies after any switch. Switching rebuilds the target policy's metadata from the resident pages ordered by last access, after discarding pages not referenced in the last 20{,}000 references (amendment A1); LFU-A and 2Q use the aged counts to place pages. After a confirmed change the controller rebaselines for $4+10$ epochs plus $B$ epochs. The persistence safeguard of the original design (several consecutive confirmed epochs) was not implemented, and the monitored features were only the miss ratio (Stage~1) and sampled reuse times (Stage~2). Table~\ref{tab:params} lists all parameters.

\subsection{Selection Protocol and Evaluation History}
\label{sec:supp_protocol}
\emph{Training.} A sweep over candidate-set size $\{4,5,6\}$, cost margin $K\in\{0,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ and evaluation delay $\{2,5,10,20\}$ was run on training traces (seed 100; 15 stable and 32 non-stable). The released \code{sweep_results.csv} records 49 configurations (4 ncand $\times$ 4 edelay $\times$ 3 kfrac grid, with one duplicate removed). It selected five candidates (adding S3-FIFO), $K=0$ and evaluation delay 10, by the lowest training miss ratio with zero stable-trace false confirmations. The training-set comprised 47 traces (15 stable + 32 non-stable); this exceeds the 26 traces (seeds 9001+) that Fig.~1 of the earlier non-retained report referenced, and the configuration grid (49 settings) exceeds the 27 settings claimed there. The selected values match the frozen configuration replayed by \code{run_main_study.sh} (\texttt{-ncand 5 -kfrac 0 -z 1.28 -edelay 10}).

\emph{Test.} The frozen configuration was evaluated on fresh traces (seed 5001; 30 stable and 80 non-stable), and no parameter was changed afterwards.

\emph{Evaluation history.} The controller was tested in two rounds (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}). In Round~1 (seeds 1001+, 110 traces) the pre-amendment configuration A0 was worse than S3-FIFO and the LPR-style baseline. Amendment A1, chiefly the recency-filtered state migration, was then designed and validated on training traces only, and the amended configuration was evaluated once on the fresh Round-2 traces. Only Round~2 is reported: the simulator, seeds and raw output of Round~1 were not retained, so its tables cannot be regenerated and none of its numbers is used. Because Round~1 informed the amendment, the reported results are the outcome of a design process with one round of test feedback, not of a single untouched test. The released \code{sweep_results.csv} contains 48 configurations evaluated on 47 training traces (15 stable + 32 non-stable, seed 100); an earlier non-retained draft reported different training-set and grid counts, and only the Round-2 results reported here are used.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
The primary metric is the miss ratio after warm-up, reported with the percentage reduction relative to LRU. Detection is evaluated on all 80 non-stable traces across four scenarios (380 true boundaries in total): a boundary is detected if a confirmed alarm occurs within 30{,}000 references after it, and the delay is the distance to that alarm. A confirmed alarm outside every such window is spurious. On stable traces, confirmations are counted over the whole trace and after reference 100{,}000, which excludes an initial transient. A reversal is a switch A$\to$B$\to$A within 60{,}000 references. Cost is the simulated wall-clock time per reference of the single released simulator on this machine; it is indicative, not a kernel measurement. Recovery time, memory overhead and full ranking agreement were not measured.

The unit of analysis is the trace (80 non-stable, 30 stable). The primary uncertainty statement is the percentile interval over traces (10{,}000 resamples for means, 5{,}000 for paired differences). Paired differences are also tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon} with Holm correction~\cite{holm} across the ten baseline comparisons, and effect sizes are mean and median paired differences with win/loss counts. Traces within a scenario share a generator and only five access primitives and five kernel models underlie all traces, so the effective number of independent units is smaller than 80 and the trace-level intervals are conditional on this design. As a coarse sensitivity check we also resample the four scenario clusters; with four clusters this yields only $\binom{7}{4}=35$ distinct resamples. It gives a DA--S3-FIFO difference of $-0.0154$ [$-0.0294$, $-0.0048$], excluding zero where the trace-level interval does not. Because 35 resamples cannot support a reliable interval, this is a sensitivity note only and is never used as the headline uncertainty statement.

\subsection{Ablation Detail}
\label{sec:supp_ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one component at a time. Shadow evaluation is essential: replacing it by a random choice raised the non-stable miss ratio from 0.453 to 0.619, worse than LRU. The other variants scored 0.453--0.456, a small range that must be read together with how often each component actually acted. Diagnostic logging of the frozen run (byte-identical outputs to the released simulator) shows the following across the 110 traces.
\begin{itemize}
\item The recency filter acted at 47 of the 213 switches, dropping 39{,}405 stale pages from the migrated resident set, without changing the mean miss ratio.
\item The KS stage was active on all 643 alarms and rejected 173 of them, leaving 470 confirmed alarms (278 true detections, 169 spurious non-stable confirmations, and 23 on stable traces) and 570 shadow evaluations. Its small ablation effect is therefore not because it never fired.
\item The cost margin never acted ($K=0$, so no cost-margin decision was made) and the dwell rule never blocked a switch (zero blocks). Under the frozen configuration these two components were inactive, so the ablation cannot estimate their benefit; counting an inactive component as ``showing no benefit'' would conflate absence of activation with absence of effect.
\item The evaluation accounting gives 470 confirmed alarms plus 110 start-policy evaluations (580) while 570 shadow evaluations are logged; the 10-evaluation difference is reported as observed in the released outputs, and the per-trace cause was not isolated in the retained artifacts.
\end{itemize}

S3-FIFO costs less than DA (28.5 vs.\ 67.4\,ns per reference) but is less accurate, so neither policy Pareto-dominates the other on the generated traces. ContShadow reached 0.423 with 27.39 shadow evaluations per trace against 6.47 for DA, a 76.4\% reduction, but it was more accurate: the saving in shadow work was \emph{not} at equal miss ratio on the generated traces.

\textbf{Diagnosis: a capacity--sampling fault.} To check for a sampling or implementation fault, each public trace was re-run with an additive diagnostic build that logs the CUSUM statistic and per-candidate shadow margins without changing control flow (identical miss ratios, switch counts and evaluation counts). S3-FIFO is a candidate (id 4), so its 0.577 standalone miss ratio was never visible to the controller. The cause is an interaction between capacity and sampling: the shadow cache holds $\Cs=pC=0.2\times 20{,}000=4{,}000$ pages, more than the roughly 1{,}100--1{,}800 distinct pages $\Dw$ in each sampled evaluation window, so all candidates incur only compulsory misses and cannot be ranked. At the synthetic-study capacity ($\Cs=200$) the same trace gives differentiated counts, S3-FIFO wins the initial selection, and DA makes one switch. The no-switch result is therefore a fault of this configuration, not evidence that candidate policies are equivalent on real workloads. \textbf{Diagnostic cap.} The cap $\Cs=\min(\mathrm{round}(pC),\mathrm{round}(f\Dw))$ with $f=1.0$ is an identity operation: it changes $\Cs$ only when $\Dw<pC$, and when all candidates fit within the sampled distinct-page set they still tie on compulsory misses. A real fix must reduce $\Cs$ below $\Dw$ or increase $\Dw$ relative to $\Cs$; this was not evaluated in the main study (see Section~S3.6 for the follow-ups).

\section{Cost Derivation and Envelope Detail}
\label{sec:supp_cost}

The measured trade-off in the replacement-quality section of the main paper is a 0.030 miss-ratio penalty for DA against a 66.0\,ns per reference time saving and 20.9 fewer shadow evaluations per trace. Whether that is a good trade depends on what a miss costs. Let $c_m$ be the cost of one miss (page fault plus service) and $c_e$ the cost of one shadow evaluation, both in nanoseconds. With the same fast path for both methods, the total time per trace is
\begin{equation}
T=N t_0+N m\,c_m+E\,c_e,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the trace length, $m$ the miss ratio, $E$ the number of shadow evaluations, and $t_0$ the per-reference cost excluding shadow work. DA beats ContShadow in total time if and only if
\begin{equation}
(m_{\mathrm{DA}}-m_{\mathrm{CS}})\,c_m+(E_{\mathrm{DA}}-E_{\mathrm{CS}})\,\frac{c_e}{N}<0,
\end{equation}
that is, $c_e>R\,c_m$ with $R=N(m_{\mathrm{DA}}-m_{\mathrm{CS}})/(E_{\mathrm{CS}}-E_{\mathrm{DA}})$. Equivalently, DA beats ContShadow whenever the miss cost is below the crossover value
\begin{equation}
c_m^{*}=c_e/R.
\label{sup:eq:breakeven}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Break-even from simulator timings.} With $m_{\mathrm{DA}}=0.453$, $m_{\mathrm{CS}}=0.423$, $E_{\mathrm{DA}}=6.47$, $E_{\mathrm{CS}}=27.39$ and $N=545{,}000$ (the average non-stable trace), \code{cost_model.py} gives $R=783.28$: one shadow evaluation must cost more than about 783 misses for DA to beat ContShadow. A 10{,}000-replicate trace-level bootstrap (\code{bootstrap_cost_model.py}, seed 20261123) gives a 95\% percentile CI of [426, 1187] for $R$. The direct measured-time comparison gives $c_m^{*}=(133.4-67.4)/(0.453-0.423)\approx 2196$\,ns $\approx 2.2\us$ per miss, with a 95\% bootstrap CI of [1.44, 4.06]\us{} (\code{cost_model_bootstrap.csv}). Both figures depend on unoptimized simulator timings; the ratio is more robust than the absolute scale, and the intervals are wide because the 80-trace sample has a heavy tail.

\textbf{Check with directly measured evaluation cost.} A timing-only build of the simulator (no diagnostic logging) measured 570 shadow evaluations on the same traces at a mean of 2.46\,ms and a median of 2.33\,ms each, about 1.4 times the 1.72\,ms implied by the time decomposition. Since $c_m^{*}$ grows with $c_e$ (Eq.~\ref{sup:eq:breakeven}), the directly measured cost shifts the DA--ContShadow crossover to $2.46\,\mathrm{ms}/783\approx 3.1\us$ per miss; the comparison with CLOCK and S3-FIFO is unchanged. Both estimates are simulator-side numbers from an unoptimized single-threaded build and should be read as an order-of-magnitude check, not as kernel latency.

\textbf{Calibration.} The reference miss cost is a user-space minor-fault measurement (\code{prototype_fault_latency.cpp}), not a kernel page-fault latency. On the evaluation machine, a first-touch minor fault on a 4\,KiB anonymous page costs 1.98\us{} and a refault after \texttt{MADV\_DONTNEED} costs 2.06\us{} (medians over seven 128\,MiB runs; a resident access costs 16\,ns). This lies just below both DA--ContShadow crossover estimates, so DA and ContShadow are close to a tie at minor-fault cost, well inside the bootstrap interval. The other $c_m$ rows in the main total-time table are illustrative parameter ranges, not measurements. At this calibration S3-FIFO is cheaper than DA; the full envelope is given below. For miss costs of 5\us{} and above (remote memory, NVMe, swap), ContShadow's lower miss ratio makes it the cheapest policy.

\textbf{Total-time envelope.} Adding the static comparators to the same model changes the interpretation. Using the measured means (main quality table) and per-reference times (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), the lower envelope is CLOCK for $c_m<0.216\us$, S3-FIFO for $0.216<c_m<2.305\us$, and ContShadow for $c_m>2.305\us$. DA is never on the envelope: its smallest excess over the best policy is 3.27\,ns/reference at $c_m\approx2.305\us$. At the measured minor-fault cost (1.98--2.06\us), S3-FIFO is cheapest; DA is about 5\,ns/reference above it and essentially tied with ContShadow. DA beats ContShadow only below 2.196\us, but S3-FIFO remains cheaper than DA until 2.517\us. A trace-level bootstrap (10{,}000 resamples, seed 20261124) gives 95\% intervals of [0.171,0.274]\us{} (CLOCK/S3-FIFO), [1.660,3.678]\us{} (S3-FIFO/ContShadow), [1.449,4.105]\us{} (DA/ContShadow), and [1.086,22.05]\us{} (DA/S3-FIFO; unstable because the miss-ratio difference is small); the minimum-gap interval is [$-28.6,40.2$]\,ns, so the ranking at the crossing is not statistically resolved. The point estimates nevertheless show no miss cost at which DA is the cheapest policy. That S3-FIFO is a candidate in DA's shadow set does not change this: DA pays the gating and evaluation overhead even when it selects S3-FIFO. An analytical async-shadow variant (\code{phase5/async\_model.csv}; evaluation fully off the request path, same miss ratios, background CPU proportional to evaluations) removes evaluation latency from the critical path but leaves the miss-ratio difference unchanged, so it does not turn DA into an accuracy win over ContShadow.

\section{Discussion and Threats: Full Detail}
\label{sec:supp_discussion}
\textbf{Hypotheses.} We framed three hypotheses (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}). H1: DA attains a lower miss ratio than the strongest fixed baseline, S3-FIFO. H2: a two-stage gate (CUSUM plus KS confirmation) improves miss ratio and detection over a single CUSUM trigger with the same sampled shadow evaluation. H3: DA's simulated per-reference cost remains competitive with fixed and adaptive baselines. A previously listed hypothesis on whether sampled shadow evaluation ranks candidates consistently with full-capacity evaluation was dropped because full ranking agreement was not measured. No cost budget or agreement threshold was specified before the study, so post hoc criteria are not treated as prospectively registered results.

\textbf{Construct validity.} Traces are generated from five synthetic primitives and five kernel-level models, not recorded from running systems. Generated phases have clean boundaries aligned with epochs, which favors detection delay, and loops larger than memory are overrepresented, which favors MRU-like heuristics. The 73.2\% detection rate and 2{,}000-reference median delay are therefore conditional on this alignment and overstate what asynchronous drift in a real system would give. Miss ratio also depends on the active policy, so the CUSUM monitor can mistake a policy-induced change for workload change; the KS stage was designed to address this but had only a small effect (0.456 without vs.\ 0.453 with) despite being active on all 643 alarms.

\textbf{Internal validity.} The configuration was selected on training traces, but one round of test feedback preceded the amendment (Section~\ref{sec:supp_protocol}), and the configuration minimizes training miss ratio, so it counts as one degree of freedom. The FIU capacity was selected on an independent pilot trace, not on an evaluated trace. Re-implementations of ARC, S3-FIFO, 2Q, LeCaR and the LPR-style heuristic may differ from the originals, baselines were not tuned per workload, and the simulator was sanity-checked against known behavior but not against reference implementations. Seeds were fixed and the shadow simulator's sampling is deterministic.

\textbf{External validity.} A single-threaded trace-driven simulator omits concurrency, memory pressure from multiple processes, reclaim interactions, writeback, I/O queueing and NUMA effects. Cost figures come from an unoptimized simulator on a shared virtual machine. Results at one nominal capacity (with sensitivity at one other) and one trace length may not transfer to other scales, and findings are limited to the evaluated workload families. The public traces are storage block/cache traces, not paging traces, and only a prefix of each was replayed. The real-program traces come from 15 instrumented kernels; six have miss ratios below 0.02 and the mean is dominated by five high-miss kernels, so 15 program-level clusters give limited power.

\textbf{Statistical conclusion validity.} The switching rule applies a normal quantile ($z=1.28$) to only four autocorrelated shadow blocks; a $t$-quantile with three degrees of freedom is about 1.64, so the nominal 90\% bound is optimistic (the $t$-quantile run gave DA 0.4586 [0.4282, 0.4888] vs.\ 0.4529 [0.4224, 0.4834]). KS $p$-values on dependent, tied reuse times are not calibrated error rates, so the confirmation stage is a heuristic gate without formal error control. Traces in one scenario share a generator, so the stated $p$-values and intervals describe variability over generated traces, not real workloads. Wilcoxon tests with ten corrected comparisons have limited power for differences of about 0.01, and small or non-significant differences (such as the KS ablation) should not be read as evidence of no effect. The post hoc trigger comparisons were corrected separately from the ten baseline comparisons.

\textbf{Scope of claims.} This paper evaluates one controller design in a single released simulator and does not establish that it outperforms the best existing replacement algorithms. A faithful kernel MGLRU baseline and an in-kernel DA replacement prototype were not built. A real asynchronous shadow-evaluation prototype was built and measured, and bounded 1M-reference prefixes of the other six public PDL families (MSR Cambridge~\cite{msr}, Alibaba, Tencent, CloudPhysics, Twitter and MetaKV) were downloaded and run; both new checks are negative for DA and are reported in Section~\ref{sec:supp_additional}. A nine-boot QEMU guest observation in Section~\ref{sec:supp_additional} produced real in-kernel fault-path durations but no reclaim event, no controller toggle and no kernel DA policy. The repository~\cite{pdl} and its non-commercial scholarly-use terms were verified, but no explicit dataset licence file was found. S4-FIFO, EACLOCK and Clock2Q+ were not implemented. The main remaining gaps are external validity and system implementation: a repair that actually brings $\Cs$ below $\Dw$ evaluated on page-level traces from running programs, and kernel-faithful replacement policies.

\subsection{QEMU guest-kernel observation (Phase 8)}

A Linux 6.6.1 guest kernel was built from source under an unprivileged user path and booted nine times under QEMU: three fresh boots each for fixed LRU (\code{lru_gen/enabled=0x0000}), fixed MGLRU (\code{0x0007}) and a userspace CUSUM controller coupled to the same sysfs knob. The build used \code{gcc-15 -std=gnu11}, locally built bison 3.8.2 and elfutils 0.191 libelf; the tarball SHA256, config options, compiler versions and controller constants (10\,ms interval, $h=5$, $\delta=0.5$, 10-epoch delay) are recorded in \code{reproduction/phase8_kernel/manifest.csv}. Six failed build/boot attempts (missing headers, C23 keyword conflicts, link order, absent dynamic loader, absent \code{/dev/null}, a filter file outside the mounted results directory and missing \code{uname}) were recorded in \code{PROTOCOL.md} and discarded before any measurement; no number from them is used here.

Function-graph tracing was restricted to the five recorded fault and reclaim functions present in the guest. Parsing the raw traces yielded 191,370 per-function duration samples in 18 condition$\times$repetition$\times$function groups, all reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:kernel_ftrace}. Only \code{handle_mm_fault} and \code{do_wp_page} produced samples. The \code{handle_mm_fault} median/p95 durations ranged from 641/1532\,ns (fixed MGLRU, repetition 1) to 761/1593\,ns (fixed LRU, repetition 1); no reclaim function was reached. The main-paper table lists the matching per-run vmstat deltas: every one of the nine runs has 131,138 minor faults, zero major faults, zero \code{workingset_refault_anon} events and zero \code{pgscan_anon}/\code{pgsteal_anon} deltas. The controller wrote no toggles in any of its three runs because its signal never became positive. The captured cgroup \code{memory.max}, \code{memory.swap.max} and \code{memory.peak} files were empty, so the guest did not demonstrate memory pressure and these runs provide no reclaim-latency measurement. This is a real in-kernel fault-path timing observation under an MGLRU-enabled guest, not an in-kernel DA replacement policy or a kernel-faithful MGLRU comparison.
