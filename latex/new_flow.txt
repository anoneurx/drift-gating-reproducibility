Drift-Aware Adaptive Page Replacement: Literature
Review and Trace-Driven Evaluation of a
Statistically Gated Policy Controller
Hunain Tahir, Muhammad Qasim, and Hussain Ahmad

Abstract—Page-replacement policies decide which memory
page is evicted under memory pressure, and classical policies
degrade when a workload changes phase. This paper reviews 15
studies from 2025–2026 on adaptive replacement, statistical drift
detection and emerging memory systems, and finds them weakly
connected: adaptive policies rarely use an explicit statistical
change decision, and drift detectors are rarely evaluated under
memory-management cost constraints. We therefore design a
drift-aware controller that separates a cheap per-reference path
from a periodic control path. It monitors the epoch miss ratio
with a CUSUM statistic, confirms a candidate change with a
two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test on sampled reuse times,
evaluates candidate policies by sampled shadow simulation, and
switches only when the estimated gain exceeds a transition-cost
margin. Against eleven baselines and an offline optimum on 110
held-out generated traces, with parameters frozen on separate
training traces, the controller’s mean miss ratio on 80 non-stable
traces was 0.410: 14.2% below LRU and significantly below
LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC (Holm-adjusted), but
not better than S3-FIFO (0.402) or a regret-driven LRU/MRU
switcher (0.381). It detected 96% of 280 phase boundaries within
about one epoch, with no false confirmations on the 30 stable
traces, and matched continuous shadow-based switching (0.410 vs
0.407) with 81% fewer shadow evaluations. Ablations show state
migration and shadow evaluation to be essential, the second-stage
confirmation to give no measurable benefit, and the controller
to cost about 6.4 times LRU per reference. The results delimit
when drift-gated policy selection is useful, but do not establish
superiority over the strongest existing methods.
Index Terms—Adaptive caching, change detection, concept
drift, page replacement, shadow simulation, trace-driven evaluation, virtual memory.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Physical memory is finite. When a process references a
page that is not resident and no free frame is available, the
memory manager must evict a resident page. The rule that
makes this choice, the page-replacement policy, affects pagefault frequency, storage traffic, tail latency and, ultimately,
application throughput. Recency-based policies such as least
recently used (LRU) and its CLOCK approximation remain the
practical default in operating systems, while the research literature offers frequency-aware, scan-resistant and self-tuning
alternatives, including the adaptive replacement cache (ARC)
[1], CLOCK with adaptive replacement (CAR) [2], the low
inter-reference recency set policy (LIRS) [3], 2Q [4] and S3FIFO [5].

No single policy is best for every access pattern. A sequential scan can flush a cache tuned for repeated reuse, a loop
slightly larger than memory can make LRU miss on every
reference, and a policy that protects a previously hot working
set can retain pages that are useless after a phase change. Real
workloads move between such regimes: a database alternates
between index lookups and table scans, a scientific code between computation kernels, and a multitasking system between
applications. The question this paper studies is therefore not
which policy is best, but whether a system can recognize
that the reference behaviour has changed and respond at an
acceptable cost.
The stakes of this choice have grown. Containerized and
multi-tenant servers interleave unrelated workloads on shared
memory, GPU and accelerator memory is oversubscribed by
multitasking and large-model serving, and tiered memory
systems make a wrong eviction more expensive because the
next level is slow or recomputation is costly [26]–[28]. Recent
studies show that adaptive and learned policies can reduce
misses substantially [15], [18], [19], but also that simple
policies such as LRU remain surprisingly competitive in some
settings [28] and that no policy dominates across workload
patterns [29]. A principled account of when adaptation pays
for itself is therefore needed, not only another adaptive algorithm.
We use workload drift to mean a statistically meaningful
change over time in the distribution or temporal structure of
page references. Two research lines address related problems.
Adaptive replacement algorithms infer from feedback which
behaviour to favour: ARC shifts capacity between recency
and frequency lists, LeCaR and CACHEUS reweight expert
policies by regret, and recent learned or learning-augmented
designs reduce the cost of learning on the eviction path [11],
[12], [15], [19]. Concept-drift research, in contrast, provides
formal change detectors with controllable error rates, such as
adaptive windowing and sequential tests [6], [7], [20]–[25].
The two lines have overlapping goals but different constraints.
A drift detector built for a machine-learning pipeline may be
too expensive, or rest on assumptions (labels, prediction errors)
that do not exist in a page-reference stream. A replacement
algorithm that adapts continuously may, conversely, switch
behaviour without any control over the rate of unnecessary
switches.
A closely related precedent exists outside operating-system
paging. Adaptive cache-mode selection for queries over raw
data [34] keeps a shadow cache of the alternative mode and

switches when a significance test favours it. That work shows
that statistically guided cache adaptation is not new; what
remains open is whether drift-gated selection among pagereplacement policies is effective once the costs of monitoring,
shadow evaluation and policy-state migration are counted, and
how it compares with strong existing replacement algorithms.
Four constraints make drift-aware replacement hard.
(a) Rate. References arrive at memory speed, so any perreference statistical work is prohibitive, and monitoring must
be sampled or summarized by epoch. (b) Missing labels and
policy-dependent signals. There is no ground truth for “the
workload changed”, and the most obvious signal, the miss
ratio, depends on the active policy and capacity, so a switch
can itself create an apparent change. (c) State. Policies keep
different metadata, and a switch must rebuild it from the
resident pages; the quality of that migration, and which pages
it retains, can decide whether a switch helps or harms. (d) Error
control. False alarms waste work and cause oscillation, while
missed changes leave a mismatched policy in place. A design
that ignores any of the four can look good on paper and fail
in operation.
This paper makes four contributions. First, we review 15
studies from 2025–2026 together with the foundational work
needed to place them, and identify where adaptive replacement, drift detection and emerging memory systems fail to
connect (Sections III and IV). Second, we specify a driftaware controller with a deliberately small set of components:
a sequential monitor on the epoch miss ratio, a distributional
confirmation test on sampled reuse times, sampled shadow
simulation of a four-policy candidate set, and a cost-aware
switching rule with a recency-filtered state migration (Section V-C). Third, we evaluate it in a trace-driven simulator
against eleven fixed and adaptive baselines (ten in the paired
tests) and an offline optimum, using a protocol that separates
training from held-out data, freezes parameters before testing,
and reports every design amendment, including one made after
a first test round (Section V). Fourth, we report where the
approach helps, where it does not, and which components
matter, through ablations, sensitivity analyses, a samplingvalidity study and a cost analysis.
Our research questions are: RQ1 How do recent adaptive
replacement policies detect and respond to changing access
behaviour? RQ2 Which drift detectors are candidates for
low-overhead use in memory management? RQ3 What do
emerging GPU, multi-tier and benchmark studies imply for
replacement under changing workloads? RQ4 Can a driftconfirmed, cost-aware controller select among replacement
policies more effectively than fixed and heuristic-adaptive
baselines under controlled phase changes? RQ1–RQ3 are
answered by the review; RQ4 is answered empirically.
We adopt a deliberately cautious evaluation stance. The
protocol fixes the design and parameters on training traces
before test traces are touched, evaluates all methods on identical traces, uses paired non-parametric statistics with multiplecomparison correction, includes an offline optimum so that the
remaining gap is visible, and reports negative and null findings
together with every design amendment. We also compare
against an Acme-style variant that applies the same shadow

evaluation without a drift gate [34], because that comparison
isolates what drift detection itself contributes. This stance is
intended to make the conclusions trustworthy even where they
are unfavourable to the proposed controller.
The answer to RQ4 is mixed, and we state it here so that the
scope of the paper is clear. On 80 held-out non-stable traces,
the controller reduced the mean miss ratio by 14.2% relative to
LRU and was significantly better than LRU, FIFO, CLOCK,
LFU-A and ARC after multiple-comparison correction. It
was not significantly different from 2Q, LeCaR, S3-FIFO or
a continuous-shadow variant, and it was worse on average
than a simple LPR-style heuristic that switches between LRU
and MRU on re-access feedback. Compared with continuous
shadow evaluation, drift gating cut shadow evaluations by 81%
at equal miss ratio. The controller’s simulated per-reference
cost, however, was 6.4 times that of LRU. We therefore do not
claim superiority over the best existing methods; we claim a
measured characterization of drift-gated policy selection, with
design lessons that apply to any adaptive replacement system
(Section V-I).
The evidence has limits that readers should keep in mind.
The experiments use generated reference traces, namely synthetic phase traces with known change points and composite
traces from five kernel-level access models, not traces collected from running operating systems. No real traces were
recorded for this study and no trace repository was used.
Public storage-block traces such as the MSR Cambridge traces
[31] do exist, but they are block traces rather than pagereference traces, and Section II-A already notes that results
on storage-block traces are not direct evidence about virtualmemory paging. Recording page traces from running programs
was simply not carried out, and we regard it as the most
important gap in this evaluation. The simulator measures algorithmic behaviour and relative cost, not kernel performance.
Section VI and Section VII discuss these limitations in detail.
The paper’s value lies in an honest, reproducible comparison
and in the specific mechanisms it isolates, such as the effect
of stale pages on policy switching, which we expect to hold
on real traces but have not verified there.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II gives conceptual background. Section III states the
review scope and synthesizes the reviewed studies, and Section IV identifies the research gaps. Section V presents the
experimental methodology and results. Section VI discusses
threats to validity, Section VII the limitations and scope of
the claims, and Section VIII concludes.
II. C ONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND
A. Page Replacement and Workload Phases
A reference trace is an ordered sequence of page identifiers.
For a capacity of C frames, a replacement policy chooses
the victim whenever a reference misses and all frames are
occupied. LRU evicts the least recently used page; LFU the
least frequently used; FIFO the oldest; CLOCK approximates
LRU with a reference bit and a circular scan; MRU evicts the
most recently used page, which is favourable for cyclic scans
larger than memory. Hybrid designs use several queues to

3

separate one-time from repeated references [1]–[5]. The miss
ratio is the fraction of references that miss; it is a necessary
but incomplete quality measure, since metadata cost, decision
cost and adaptation speed also matter. Results on storageblock traces are cache-replacement results and are not direct
evidence about virtual-memory paging.
Workload phases differ in working-set size, reuse-distance
distribution, sequentiality and frequency skew. Drift may be
abrupt, gradual or recurring [6]. Phase changes also interact
with policy state: a policy that retains pages of the previous
phase (for example MRU, which pins the oldest resident
pages) can perform far worse than its steady-state behaviour
suggests until that state is flushed. This interaction is central
to the design and results of this paper.
B. Workload Drift and Statistical Detection
Concept-drift research distinguishes changes in the input
distribution, in the input–output relationship, or both [6]. A
page-reference stream has no labels, so a controller must
monitor observable summaries such as the miss ratio or reusetime distribution. Adaptive windowing (ADWIN) tests whether
sub-windows have different means [7]; Page–Hinkley and
CUSUM procedures accumulate deviations from a reference
level and signal when the sum exceeds a threshold [30];
two-sample tests such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test
compare distributions. Each has assumptions about sample
size, independence and repeated testing that must be addressed
in an implementation. Because memory operations occur at
very high rates, a detector for this setting must avoid perreference heavy computation and must balance detection delay,
false alarms, memory use and runtime cost.
C. Earlier Adaptive Replacement and Cache Modelling
SEQ [8] detects long fault sequences and handles them
separately, and EELRU [9] adjusts its eviction point from
recency-distribution evidence. ARC [1] balances recency and
frequency using ghost-list hits, CAR [2] transfers the idea to
CLOCK, LIRS [3] uses inter-reference recency, and 2Q [4]
separates newly admitted from re-referenced pages. S3-FIFO
[5] shows that FIFO queues with a small admission queue can
match or beat more complex designs. ACME [10], LeCaR
[11] and CACHEUS [12] mix expert policies using regret
or learned feedback. Miniature simulation [13] and spatially
hashed sampling [14] approximate larger cache configurations
cheaply, which makes on-demand evaluation of alternative
policies feasible, although sampling can distort temporal structure and policy rankings. These works establish that workloadsensitive replacement is long-standing; the proposed controller
does not claim that adaptation itself is new.
III. R EVIEW S COPE AND A PPROACH
This is a focused narrative review, not a systematic review
or meta-analysis. The study set was selected by the authors
to cover adaptive replacement, drift detection and emerging
memory or cache settings, and comprises 15 peer-reviewed
publications and preprints from 2025–2026 plus foundational

work needed to explain the lineage. One study [20] first
appeared as a preprint in December 2024 and was published
at AAAI 2025. No preregistered search protocol, duplicate
screening or quality scoring was applied, so the review does
not claim systematic completeness, and several included works
are preprints. Results reported in the studies are not pooled or
normalized because workloads, hardware and metrics differ.

A. Adaptive and Learning-Based Replacement
3L-Cache [15] reduces the cost of learned eviction by
moving learning and inference off the critical path, evaluated
on 4,855 CDN, key-value and block traces, but its overhead
remains above LRU. LPR [16] uses re-access feedback to
switch among policies at runtime, for example from LRU to
MRU, with a heuristic rather than statistical switching signal.
EACLOCK [17] adapts a CLOCK-style policy from hitrate feedback for skewed and shifting workloads. Clock2Q+
[18] targets correlated metadata references in VMware vSAN
and reports up to 28.5% lower miss ratio than S3-FIFO on
metadata traces. S4-FIFO [19] learns configuration parameters
for a FIFO-family heuristic rather than every eviction, and, like
3L-Cache, separates a fast data path from slower control logic.

B. Drift Detection
PUDD [20] detects drift from prediction uncertainty but
needs a predictive model. A label-free statistical-processcontrol detector [21] stresses power and false-positive control
but is evaluated by simulation of model-monitoring scenarios.
Dynamic threshold determination [22] adapts alarm thresholds
after each alarm, at the cost of maintaining parallel candidate
models temporarily. A transformer–autoencoder hybrid [23]
and an HTM-plus-SPRT framework [24] offer richer temporal
modelling but impose training or univariate-data assumptions.
ADWIN-U [25] adapts adaptive windowing to unsupervised
settings. None is evaluated on page-reference streams or at
memory-management rates.

C. GPU, Multi-Tier and Benchmark Studies
MSched [26] predicts the next task’s working set from
kernel launch arguments to migrate GPU memory proactively,
which relies on predictability that CPU page streams lack.
gpu_ext [27] makes GPU-driver policies replaceable eBPF
programs, an architectural mechanism for safe policy updates.
The LLM prefix-cache study [28] finds LRU surprisingly
competitive, cautioning against assuming that sophisticated
policies always win. A stale-tolerant cache benchmark [29]
finds that no policy dominates across four synthetic workload
patterns. Finally, Acme [34] is the closest precedent for
statistical policy switching: it simulates the alternative caching
mode through shadow entries and switches when a one-tailed
t-test at the 0.05 level favours it, reporting execution time
within 16% of the optimal mode in the worst case and 4% on
average on TPC-H workloads.

IV. P REVIOUS R ESEARCH G APS
Table I summarizes the 15 reviewed studies and the closest
prior art by research area, contribution and limitation relative
to the goal of low-overhead, drift-aware page replacement. The
limitations are framed against that goal; they are not claims
that the original studies fail at their own objectives, and some
are inferred from each study’s scope. Reported results are not
recomputed or compared across studies.
The reviewed work establishes that adaptive replacement is
well studied [1]–[5], [8]–[12]; that learning-based policies can
respond online to access patterns [15]–[19]; that statistical drift
detection offers explicit change evidence [6], [7], [20]–[25];
and that cache-mode selection has used shadow evaluation and
significance testing [34]. Four gaps follow.
Gap 1: detectors on reference streams. Generic drift detectors are rarely evaluated on page-reference feature streams at
memory-management sampling rates and overhead budgets.
Gap 2: statistical gating versus heuristic adaptation. Adaptive
page-replacement studies such as LPR [16] already respond
to changing patterns, but no reviewed study compares explicit
statistical change confirmation against such heuristics in a
controlled experiment. Gap 3: transition costs. The costs of
detector execution, shadow simulation, policy-state preparation
and post-switch warm-up are not consistently reported together
with miss ratio and detection delay. Gap 4: comparability.
Studies use different workloads, metrics and system contexts,
so their results cannot be compared as though from a shared
benchmark.

E. Replacement Quality
Table VI and Figs. 3 and 4 give the main result. On the 80
non-stable traces the controller’s mean miss ratio was 0.410
(95% CI 0.387–0.433), 14.2% below LRU (0.477). The offline
optimum reached 0.223, so every online method leaves a large
gap. The ranking of the other methods was: LPR-style 0.381,
S3-FIFO 0.402, ContShadow 0.407, DA 0.410, LeCaR 0.421,
2Q 0.431, ARC 0.449 and LRU 0.477. MRU is dominated
except on loops. On stable traces the controller (0.332) was
close to the best fixed policies (S3-FIFO 0.342, 2Q 0.344),
mostly because of its start-up policy selection.
By scenario, the controller was competitive on abrupt traces
(0.412, level with ContShadow at 0.411 and below S3-FIFO
at 0.458) but clearly weaker on gradual drift (0.474 versus
0.407 for S3-FIFO and 0.405 for LPR-style). In the gradual
scenario the reuse-time distribution moves slowly, so the KS
confirmation was often not triggered while the policy mixture
was changing; a variant without that stage reached 0.459 on
gradual traces against 0.474 (a per-scenario value not tabulated
in Table VIII). On composite kernel traces all methods except
MRU were within about 0.05 of each other.
Table VII reports paired comparisons; negative differences
favour the controller. It was significantly better than LRU,
FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC after Holm correction

(adjusted p ≤ 0.035). Fig. 5 shows the paired differences.
Intervals and tests answer different questions: the bootstrap
interval describes uncertainty in the mean difference, whereas
the Wilcoxon test depends on the ranks of the per-trace
differences. For 2Q the interval for the mean excludes zero
(−0.038 to −0.006) while the adjusted p-value is 0.288, and
for LPR-style the interval excludes zero in the unfavourable
direction while the adjusted p-value is 0.332. Such patterns
can arise when differences are skewed, and the intervals are
not corrected for multiplicity or for clustering of traces by
scenario.
Against 2Q (−0.0217, adjusted p = 0.288), LeCaR, S3FIFO (+0.0080, adjusted p = 0.281) and ContShadow the
differences were not significant. The LPR-style baseline had
a lower mean miss ratio (mean difference +0.0287, bootstrap
interval excluding zero) although the Wilcoxon test on the
median difference of 0.000 was not significant (adjusted p =
0.332); the distribution is skewed, because LPR-style wins by
a wide margin on loop-containing phases and ties elsewhere. In
round 1 (design A0) the controller was significantly worse than
S3-FIFO (+0.0135, p < 0.001), so the amendment narrowed
but did not remove the gap.

G. Detection, Switching and Cost
The monitor and confirmation stages detected 269 of 280
phase boundaries (96.1%) in abrupt, recurring and composite
traces. The median delay was 1,999 references, one epoch,
with essentially no spread; this should be read with care,
because all boundaries in these traces coincide with epoch
boundaries, which favours detection speed.
Two different false-alarm measures are reported and should
not be conflated. (i) On the 30 stable traces, a false confirmation is any confirmed alarm after reference 100,000, because
no true change exists; none occurred. (ii) On the 60 abrupt,
recurring and composite traces, a spurious confirmation is
a confirmed alarm outside every 30,000-reference boundary

window; 11 occurred. Measure (ii) is counted per trace set,
can include within-phase structure such as sliding windows
and mixtures, and is not a per-reference rate. For (i), the 30
stable traces contribute 30×(300,000−100,000) = 6.0 million
references after the exclusion, so with zero events the rule-ofthree upper bound is 3/6.0 = 0.50 false confirmations per
million references.
The controller made 2.12 switches per non-stable trace
(LPR-style 1.84, ContShadow 2.98, DA-noCost 3.94). Table VIII reports 1.70 switches per trace over all 110 traces;
multiplying out, 1.70 × 110 = 187 switches in total, of which
2.12×80 ≈ 170 fall on the 80 non-stable traces. The remaining
17 therefore occurred on the 30 stable traces, about 0.57 each.
Stable traces do switch, but only when the start-up shadow
evaluation or a confirmed alarm selects a policy other than
the one already active, so the two switch figures differ solely
in their denominator. There were no A→B→A reversals in
those 187 switches.
Cost is the main drawback. Simulated time per reference
was 10.1 ns for LRU, 9.0 for CLOCK, 13.3 for 2Q, 13.3
for S3-FIFO, 16.5 for LPR-style, 65.1 for DA, 141.4 for
ContShadow and 165.7 for LeCaR (Fig. 7). The controller thus
costs 6.4 times LRU, but 54% less than continuous shadow
evaluation. Shadow simulation accounted for 31% of its time,
confirmation tests 3% and switching 3%; the remaining 63%
is the always-on fast-path work (hash sampling, per-page
counters and periodic count aging), which the prototype does
not optimize. Fig. 7 plots miss ratio against simulated cost; DA
lies between the cheap heuristics and the continuous-shadow
methods in cost but not in miss ratio. These timings come
from an unoptimized simulator on a shared machine and only
indicate relative magnitude. Under any budget below about 6
times LRU, H3 would not be satisfied by this implementation.

I. Hypothesis Outcomes and Design Lessons
Four lessons emerge. First, the state of the replacement
policy is as important as the choice of policy: switching to

11

a policy that pins old pages, without first discarding stale
pages, can be catastrophic, and a shadow window that includes pre-change references misjudges such policies (the two
mechanisms behind amendment A1; the migration filter alone
is worth 0.022 in miss ratio). Second, evaluating candidates
before switching is indispensable (random choice is no better
than LRU), but it need not be continuous: gating it on
confirmed drift removed 81% of the evaluations at equal miss
ratio. Third, a cost margin on switching reduces switching by
about half without a measurable loss. Fourth, a simple regretdriven LRU/MRU heuristic beat the full controller on average,
mostly because loops larger than memory are common in
the test workloads and MRU solves them after a short LRU
flush; any real-trace evaluation should check how common
such patterns are. Together these results support the integration
hypothesis only in a limited form: drift gating is a cost-saving
mechanism for statistical policy selection, not yet an accuracy
advantage over the best existing replacement algorithms.
VI. T HREATS TO VALIDITY
Construct validity. The traces are generated: five synthetic
access primitives and five kernel-level access models, not
traces recorded from running systems. Generated phases have
clean boundaries aligned with the controller’s epochs, which
favours detection delay, and workload types such as loops
larger than memory are overrepresented relative to their frequency in practice, which favours policies such as MRU and
regret-driven LRU/MRU switching. Miss ratio is also partly
determined by the active policy, so the Stage-1 monitor can
mistake a policy-induced change for workload change; the
reuse-time confirmation stage was designed to address this,
but our data did not show a measurable benefit from it.
Internal validity. The design was amended once after the
first test round, using information from that round. We mitigated this by validating the amendment on training traces
only and evaluating on a fresh trace set, by keeping all
parameters fixed, and by reporting both rounds; a reader should
nevertheless treat the amended design as having had one
additional degree of freedom. Re-implementations of ARC,
S3-FIFO, 2Q, LeCaR and the LPR-style heuristic may differ
from the original implementations, and baselines were not
tuned per workload, which can favour or penalize them. The
simulator was sanity-checked against known behaviour but not
against reference implementations. Seeds were fixed and the
shadow simulator’s sampling is deterministic.
External validity. A single-threaded trace-driven simulator
omits concurrency, memory pressure from multiple processes,
reclaim interactions, writeback, I/O queueing and NUMA
effects. Cost figures come from an unoptimized simulator
on a shared virtual machine and should be read as relative
magnitudes. Results at one nominal capacity (with sensitivity
at two others) and one trace length may not transfer to other
scales. Findings are limited to the evaluated workload families.
Statistical conclusion validity. The switching rule applies a
normal quantile (z = 1.28) to only four autocorrelated shadow
blocks; a t-quantile with three degrees of freedom is about
1.64, so the nominal 90% bound is optimistic. KS p-values on

dependent, tied reuse times are not calibrated error rates, so the
confirmation stage provides a heuristic gate rather than formal
error control. Traces within a scenario share a generator, so the
effective number of independent workloads is smaller than 80,
and the stated p-values and intervals describe variability over
generated traces, not over real workloads. Wilcoxon tests with
ten corrected comparisons have limited power for differences
of about 0.01 in mean miss ratio. The H3 budget and H4
threshold were not pre-registered. Several secondary results
(for example the KS stage having no measurable effect) are
non-significant differences and should not be read as evidence
of no effect.
VII. L IMITATIONS AND S COPE OF C LAIMS
This paper evaluates one controller design in a simulator.
It does not establish that the controller outperforms the best
existing replacement algorithms: S3-FIFO and a simple LPRstyle heuristic had lower mean miss ratios, and the difference
to LPR-style is not removed by the amendment. The comparison omits several strong published methods (CAR, LIRS,
CACHEUS, 3L-Cache, S4-FIFO, EACLOCK and Clock2Q+)
because implementations were unavailable, so the ranking
among methods applies only to the compared set. The candidate set of the controller is small and excludes ARC and
S3-FIFO; including them could change the results. The implemented design omits the persistence safeguard and uses only
two monitored features. Recovery time and memory overhead
were not measured, and the controller performs poorly on
gradual drift in this configuration.
Of these, the absence of recorded page traces is by far
the most significant: every result in Section V comes from
five access primitives and five kernel models, so conclusions
about detection speed, false-confirmation rates and policy
ranking are conclusions about generated workloads. Pagereference traces can be recorded from real programs with
modest instrumentation, and their absence here is the primary
limitation of this study.
The literature review is narrative, not systematic, and its
limitations in Table I are partly inferred. The study makes
no claim about production kernels, real application traces, or
latency. We claim only (i) a characterization of drift-gated
policy selection in a controlled setting, (ii) evidence that state
migration and shadow evaluation are essential components,
(iii) evidence that drift gating can reduce shadow-evaluation
work by 81% at equal miss ratio, and (iv) a reproducible
protocol, including its amendment history, for testing such
controllers.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
This paper reviewed 15 studies from 2025–2026 on adaptive
replacement, statistical drift detection and emerging memory
systems, and found that the three areas are weakly connected.
To test whether closing the gap pays off, we implemented a
drift-gated controller with CUSUM monitoring, KS confirmation, sampled shadow simulation and a cost-aware switching
rule, and evaluated it against eleven baselines (ten in the paired
tests) and an offline optimum on 110 held-out generated traces

12

after freezing parameters on training data and re-testing on
fresh traces.
The controller reduced the mean miss ratio on non-stable
traces by 14.2% relative to LRU and was significantly better
than LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, LFU-A and ARC. It was not better
than S3-FIFO or an LPR-style LRU/MRU heuristic, and it
cost about 6.4 times LRU per reference in the simulator.
Detection was fast and clean on abrupt phase changes, and
drift gating reduced shadow-evaluation work by 81% relative
to continuous evaluation at the same miss ratio. The ablations
identify recency-filtered state migration and shadow evaluation
as essential, and show that the second-stage confirmation did
not help here. Statistically gated policy selection is therefore
a viable but not yet superior alternative to existing adaptive
replacement.
Future work should (i) repeat the evaluation on recorded
traces from real systems (for example the MSR Cambridge
block traces [31], noting that storage-block traces are cachereplacement evidence, not virtual-memory evidence), (ii) include the missing baselines and a wider candidate set that
contains ARC and S3-FIFO, (iii) combine drift gating with
regret feedback to handle gradual change, (iv) reduce alwayson overhead and measure memory cost, recovery time and
kernel-level latency, and (v) pre-register overhead budgets and
agreement thresholds before testing.
